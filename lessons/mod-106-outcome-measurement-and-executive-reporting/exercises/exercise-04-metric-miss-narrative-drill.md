# Exercise 04 — Metric Miss Narrative Drill

**Estimated effort:** 2 hours
**Format:** three written miss narratives in the five-part structure, plus a short reflection
**Grounding chapters:** [02 — Authoring the Executive Scorecard — Weekly, Monthly, Quarterly](../02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md), [03 — Writing for Executives — Board Updates, LT Briefings, Strategic Memos](../03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md)

## Objective

Train the reflex of writing an honest miss narrative — target, actual, why, what is being done, what was learned — against three different miss shapes you will actually see in a portfolio. The miss narrative is the single highest-leverage writing in the module. A program that writes credible miss narratives builds credibility across quarters; a program that writes euphemistic ones loses it. This drill forces the shape until it is automatic.

## Prerequisites

- [Chapter 02](../02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md) read, with the five-part miss narrative structure and the three miss-section failure modes (euphemism, scope-swap, attribution flight) available.
- [Chapter 03](../03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md) read, with the misses-in-the-lead discipline.
- The three vignettes below treated as fictional illustrative misses; the specific numbers are plausible scene-setting, not sourced.

## Problem statement

Three rollouts in your portfolio are missing their Q3 targets. The monthly deep-dive goes to the leadership team in three days. You need three miss narratives, each in the five-part Chapter 02 structure, ready for Section 3 of the deep-dive. The LT has a CFO who has seen this pattern before at other companies, a CIO who is skeptical of AI-adjacent program claims, and the sponsoring CAIO who is counting on these misses being delivered credibly.

### Vignette A — the workflow-fit miss

> **Legal drafting rollout, Q3 target 25% reduction in time-to-first-draft, actual 9% reduction.**
>
> Pilot charter target was 25% reduction in median time-to-first-draft on comparable cross-border advisory matters, measured on the pilot cohort of 180 associates, versus pre-rollout baseline of 4.2 hours.
>
> Q3 actual: 9% reduction (median 3.8 hours, n=174 over 12 weeks). Variance by matter type is wide: retained-client advisory saw 24% reduction, new-matter drafting saw 2%.
>
> Six of eight interviews with senior associates in the new-matter cohort surfaced the same pattern: the tool's context-loading is too light for first-time matter research, leading to under-prepared drafts that require extensive rework at review.

### Vignette B — the measurement-issue miss

> **Support copilot rollout, Q3 target 18% reduction in median ticket handle time, actual flat.**
>
> Pilot charter target was 18% reduction in median handle time across tier-1 support tickets, measured on the pilot cohort of 220 agents across two product lines, against a pre-rollout baseline of 7.4 minutes.
>
> Q3 actual: flat (median 7.4 minutes, n=204 agents over 14 weeks). Workflow interviews suggest the tool is being used heavily but the handle-time measurement includes a 90-second escalation delay that the tool cannot affect.
>
> The analyst has identified that the handle-time clock includes the queue-to-agent-pickup delay. If the measurement is adjusted to agent-on-ticket time only, a preliminary rerun shows a 14% reduction. The methodology question is whether to re-baseline mid-quarter (changing the metric mid-flight) or carry the current miss and change the metric at the quarter boundary.

### Vignette C — the change-management-lag miss

> **Meeting-notes rollout, Q3 target 65% weekly active use among in-scope operators, actual 38%.**
>
> Pilot charter target was 65% weekly active use among 900 in-scope operators by end of Q3, measured by weeks in which the operator initiated at least one tool-generated note.
>
> Q3 actual: 38% (approximately 342 of 900 operators active in the final week of Q3). The adoption curve shape ([mod-105 Chapter 07](../../mod-105-champion-networks-and-change-management/07-measuring-change-management-health.md) — flat-line pattern) suggests the rollout has not found workflow fit *and* that the champion network in two of three business units has gone quiet.

## Requirements

For each vignette, produce the five-part miss narrative from [Chapter 02](../02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md):

1. **Target.** What the target was, quoted from the pilot charter.
2. **Actual.** What the actual value is, with denominator.
3. **Why.** The diagnosis. One dominant diagnosis plus a secondary note if applicable.
4. **What is being done.** The specific intervention, the named owner, the next review date.
5. **Learned.** What this miss teaches at the program level — not at the rollout level.

Each narrative is 150-250 words. Longer than that is prose; shorter is likely under-diagnosed.

Note: Vignette B is deliberately a measurement issue, not a tool issue. Writing it honestly is harder than it looks — the easy move is to re-baseline silently and report the 14% as the actual, which is the moving-goalpost pattern from [Chapter 01](../01-the-measurement-stack-at-org-scope.md). The honest move is to carry the flat result, explain the measurement question, and propose the methodology change as an ask in Section 8 of the deep-dive. Write Vignette B accordingly.

After the three narratives, add a short reflection (three to five sentences):

- Which of the three misses was hardest to write without sliding into euphemism, scope-swap, or attribution flight.
- One sentence per narrative identifying the "learned" line you are most uncertain about, and why.
- One sentence on what you would propose to add to the deep-dive's Section 5 (qualitative ground truth) that would reinforce one of the three diagnoses.

## Starter guidance

- The "learned" line is the hardest part of the discipline. It cannot be "we should have measured better" — that is attribution flight in miniature. A credible learned line names a *program-level* change: a new rule for pilot design, a new interview question for next quarter, a new attribution check in the ROI methodology.
- For Vignette B, the temptation is to write the actual as 14%. Resist it; the actual is flat, with a measurement question attached as an ask. The honest version will read as self-critical; it will also read as the most credible of the three to the CFO.
- "What is being done" in Vignette A must specifically reference the re-scope (rollout continues for retained-client advisory, new-matter drafting goes back to a selection re-examination) and the owner by role, not name. Do the same for the other two.
- "Learned" lines should ladder up. Each one teaches something that will shape program-level behaviour: pilot design, cohort decomposition, measurement discipline, change-management handoff.
- Use the Chapter 03 confidence-band discipline on the actual values in Vignette B's methodology-adjusted number. Even a corrected measurement comes with uncertainty.

## Deliverables

- `miss-narratives.md` with:
  - Three miss narratives (A, B, C) each in the five-part structure.
  - The short reflection section.

## Acceptance criteria

- Each miss narrative contains all five parts in the specified order with the specified labels.
- None of the narratives slide into the three Chapter 02 failure modes (euphemism, scope-swap, attribution flight). A reader can tell which failure mode you avoided for each.
- Each "learned" line is a *program-level* lesson, not a rollout-level lesson. "We should have scoped new-matter work separately" is program-level if it generalizes; "we should have talked to the senior partners sooner" is not.
- Vignette B's actual is reported as flat, not as the methodology-adjusted 14%. The methodology correction is proposed as an ask, not quietly applied.
- Each "what is being done" line names the owner by role (not personal name) and a specific next review date.
- The reflection names one specific "learned" line you are uncertain about and why; "all of them were hard" does not count.

## Stretch goals

- Write the *second* miss narrative for Vignette B — the one that goes into the Q4 deep-dive, after the methodology change is approved and the re-baselined measurement produces the Q4 actual. The second narrative is harder than the first because it carries the credibility implication of the mid-flight methodology change.
- Draft the three-sentence investment-ask (Section 8 of the deep-dive) that corresponds to each of the three misses. The ask must have a decision owner, a decision-by date, and a cost-and-value framing per Chapter 02.
- Rewrite one of the three misses as it would appear in the quarterly board-ready review (as opposed to the monthly deep-dive). The two shapes differ: the quarterly is denser, more learned-line, and composes with the hit list. See [Chapter 02](../02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md).

## Why this exercise matters

A program's credibility with executives is built on the *second* miss, not the first. Executives who read a program's deep-dive believe the hits if the misses were credible last month. The miss narrative is how that credibility is signalled, every month, in five specific parts. The drill is the reps — most enablement leads need to write ten to fifteen of these before the shape is automatic. Three well-written miss narratives, in which you can defend every editorial choice to a skeptical CFO, is the floor this exercise is clearing.
