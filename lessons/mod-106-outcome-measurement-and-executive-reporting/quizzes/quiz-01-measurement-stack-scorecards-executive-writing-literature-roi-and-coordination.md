# Quiz 01 — The Measurement Stack, the Three-Cadence Scorecard, Executive Writing, the Empirical Literature, ROI with Finance, and the Three-Tier Coordination

**Format:** multiple choice · **Questions:** 12 · **Estimated effort:** 25–30 minutes
**Grounding:** [Chapter 01](../01-the-measurement-stack-at-org-scope.md), [Chapter 02](../02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md), [Chapter 03](../03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md), [Chapter 04](../04-reading-the-empirical-ai-productivity-literature-for-method-not-numbers.md), [Chapter 05](../05-partnering-with-finance-on-cost-model-and-roi.md), [Chapter 06](../06-coordinating-with-the-analyst-and-the-team-manager.md)

## How to take this quiz

Pick the single best answer for each question. Each question tests whether you can *apply* a chapter idea to a concrete situation — paraphrasing the chapter is not enough. Answer key with short explanations is at the bottom; resist the urge to read it until you have your own answers written down.

---

### Q1. The AI steering committee's quarterly pack opens with a headline chart: "AI tools in production: Q1 = 3, Q2 = 7, Q3 = 12, Q4 = 18." The count has grown every quarter since the program started. Under Chapter 01's four named theatre patterns at org scope, this headline is specifically:

- **A.** The cherry-picked rollout — only the strongest rollouts are featured, the rest have disappeared from the exec surface.
- **B.** The rollout-count scoreboard — the count rises every quarter because rollouts accumulate; it would also rise in a program producing no business value at all. The headline is a portfolio-scale equivalent of "95% of licensed users logged in."
- **C.** The moving goalpost — the target metric changed mid-flight after the original one did not move.
- **D.** The vendor-number headline — the number came from a vendor whitepaper and was presented as the organization's own measurement.

### Q2. An enablement lead proposes, for the quarterly review's headline, a single portfolio-level outcome: "AI-induced median time savings across the portfolio: 18%." The portfolio contains drafting workflows, meeting-notes workflows, support-ticket resolution workflows, and code-review workflows. Under Chapter 01's two aggregation disciplines, this headline is most clearly:

- **A.** Defensible, provided a 90% confidence band is attached.
- **B.** Defensible, provided the denominator (operators × weeks) is published alongside.
- **C.** A category error — "time per task" across drafting and support-ticket workflows is a different-workflow statistic; the common statistic is a category error. The honest move is four-to-six category rollups, with a grand total (if one appears) explicitly a weighted composite with the weights published.
- **D.** Defensible, because a single number is what a board reads first and the sub-rollups can live in the appendix.

### Q3. A VP who is not in the enablement lead's operating circle asks to be added to the weekly rollup distribution list. Under Chapter 02's cadence discipline, the most grounded response is:

- **A.** Add the VP and recut the rollup into executive language for distribution.
- **B.** Decline directly. The weekly rollup is an operating document for the enablement lead and the small set of partners who allocate next week's work; a VP asking to see it is a signal that the monthly deep-dive is not landing, which is a separate conversation to open.
- **C.** Add the VP; the weekly rollup is designed to serve all stakeholders.
- **D.** Add the VP but remove the misses section when the VP is on the distribution list.

### Q4. The exec sponsor returns an edit to this month's deep-dive: move Section 3 (Misses and their reasons) to the end of the document as an "operational appendix," because "leading with misses sets the wrong tone for the leadership team." Under Chapter 02's misses-first discipline, the grounded response is:

- **A.** Accept the edit; the sponsor reads the leadership team better than the enablement lead.
- **B.** Accept, and add a vendor benchmark to the hits section to balance the tone.
- **C.** Decline the move. The role's credibility with executives is built on the *second* month: executives who read a credible misses section early come to believe the hits that follow. Program leaders who bury misses teach executives to discount the hits — the alternative, all wins every quarter, has a short half-life.
- **D.** Accept, and move the misses into a side memo only the governance council sees.

### Q5. A miss narrative on the Legal drafting rollout reads, in the five-part structure: target 25% reduction; actual 9% reduction (n=174, 12 weeks); diagnosis workflow-fit issue in new-matter drafting confirmed in 6 of 8 interviews; intervention a re-scope to retained-client advisory with a named owner and 15 October next-review date; learned "we should have measured better before committing." Under Chapter 02's miss-narrative discipline, the weakest part of the narrative is:

- **A.** The target statement — it should quote the pilot charter target language verbatim.
- **B.** The actual value — the denominator is understated.
- **C.** The diagnosis — a single dominant diagnosis is required even for multi-cause misses.
- **D.** The *learned* line. "We should have measured better" is a rollout-level non-commitment that reads as attribution-flight; a credible learned line names a *program-level* change — a new rule for pilot design, a new interview question, a new attribution check — that will shape next quarter's rollouts. The learned line is the most important sentence in the miss narrative.

### Q6. A board update opens: "The AI-powered productivity tools we deployed this year have reached 78% of the workforce, representing a transformative milestone in our digital transformation journey." Under Chapter 03's three named theatre failure modes in executive communication, this opening carries which combination?

- **A.** Engagement lede only — the paragraph opens with a usage number.
- **B.** Vendor voice only — the paragraph uses vendor-marketing language.
- **C.** Strategy abstraction only — the paragraph makes no specific commitment.
- **D.** Engagement lede *and* vendor voice — the first sentence opens with a usage percentage (the mod-102 Chapter 04 adoption-percentage failure in executive-communication dress), and the surrounding language ("transformative milestone," "digital transformation journey") is indistinguishable from a vendor whitepaper. The remediation is an outcome metric against target in the lead, in the organization's own language.

### Q7. A strategic memo to the CFO, a single page in length, carries this sentence as its forward-looking commitment: "We will continue to drive AI-powered productivity across the enterprise and are confident in the Q4 trajectory." Under Chapter 03's structural moves and theatre patterns, the grounded remediation is:

- **A.** Add a BLUF paragraph before the sentence to clarify the ask.
- **B.** Replace the sentence with a specific, measurable, date-anchored commitment — e.g., "In Q4, the retained-client drafting rollout will move from an 11% to a 22% reduction against baseline on the pilot cohort of 180 associates, with the re-scope decision reviewable at week 6." The *strategy abstraction* pattern protects the writer over one quarter and erodes credibility over four.
- **C.** Add a vendor benchmark to anchor the trajectory claim.
- **D.** Move the sentence into the appendix.

### Q8. An enablement lead opens the quarterly review's drafting-workflow section with: "GitHub's own research (Peng et al.) reports a 55.8% median reduction in task completion time; our rollout targets a conservative 25% for Q4." Under Chapter 04's reading discipline, the dominant error is:

- **A.** 25% is too low a target; the published number should set a higher floor.
- **B.** The 55.8% headline is an effect from a controlled experiment on 95 self-selected developers implementing one bounded HTTP-server task over a short session. Transplanting the number across different task, population, cadence, and baseline is exactly the vendor-number-headline failure that Chapter 04's reading discipline is designed to prevent — cite the Peng *method* (pre-registered primary outcome, telemetry-as-texture), not the Peng number.
- **C.** 25% is too high a target for a Q4 window.
- **D.** Peng et al. is published research, not vendor marketing, so no reading discipline applies.

### Q9. A support-copilot rollout reports a 14% median reduction in handle time at week 4 of the pilot. A board member asks whether the result "will hold up over time." Under Chapter 04's five named threats to validity, the most relevant threat — and the most useful methodological response — is:

- **A.** Selection into use — report against the mandatory in-scope cohort rather than the opted-in subset, and note the self-selection bias in the methodology appendix.
- **B.** Measurement drift — operators are gaming the metric by shortening handle time at quality cost; pair the productivity metric with a sampled quality metric to catch it.
- **C.** Attribution to AI specifically — a concurrent training-program refresh and a workflow redesign may account for the movement independently of the tool; note the concurrent changes in the methodology appendix.
- **D.** Novelty effects — early adoption often shows exaggerated effects that dampen at steady state. The response is to report longer trajectories (the six-month measurement is more credible than the one-month) and carry the Brynjolfsson et al. longitudinal frame explicitly, so the trajectory shape is on the scorecard rather than a snapshot.

### Q10. An enablement lead builds the Q3 ROI denominator by summing the vendor-license invoices and the CoE operating budget line, arriving at $4.2M. Under Chapter 05's four-component denominator, what is most likely missing — and why is it the first thing a Finance partner will challenge?

- **A.** Nothing — tool licenses plus CoE operating budget is the full denominator at program scope.
- **B.** Opportunity cost of not adopting a different tool, and the compounding three-year license escalation.
- **C.** Embedded enablement-partner FTE time (the fraction of senior engineers' and champions' time spent on enablement work, loaded at the organization's fully-loaded FTE multiplier) and downstream support cost (help-desk tickets generated by AI-tool support, security-review time, legal-review time on policy updates, incident-response time when a rollout surfaces harm). The honest denominator is not smaller than it is; omitting these produces a ROI that will not survive the standing quarterly review.
- **D.** One-time pilot costs from prior quarters, re-amortised across the current quarter.

### Q11. Last quarter's ROI line in the quarterly review was 2.8x. This quarter the attribution fraction has been raised from 40% to 55%, with the authoring note that "the prior number was feeling conservative"; no new cohort comparison, workflow-interview update, or literature re-reading is cited. Under Chapter 05's two named ROI theatre patterns, this is specifically:

- **A.** A legitimate methodological refinement — attribution fractions should tighten as the program matures.
- **B.** Compounding-ROI-forever — the gain is being projected forward as if it scales linearly across years.
- **C.** Reverse-engineered ROI — the target number is being chosen first, and the attribution fraction adjusted until the ratio lands. The tell is exactly this: the attribution fraction changes between quarters without a corresponding change in the evidence that would justify it. The methodology appendix is being written to support the number, not to produce it.
- **D.** A sensitivity-analysis update that happens to fall out this way.

### Q12. A team manager of a business unit volunteers to author the Section 3 miss narrative for their team's Q3 drafting rollout in the monthly deep-dive, arguing "I know the diagnosis best, I should write it." Under Chapter 06's three-tier role-boundary discipline, the grounded response is:

- **A.** Accept; the team manager knows the diagnosis.
- **B.** Decline with a specific split. The team manager is the source of the diagnosis and provides the texture; the exec-facing miss narrative is the enablement lead's writing. Asking a team manager to author an exec-facing miss narrative pulls them into executive communication without the skill, the sponsor relationship, or the portfolio context — a named failure shape in Chapter 06's "what the enablement lead does not ask the team manager to do."
- **C.** Accept, and route the draft to Finance for review before it goes in the deep-dive.
- **D.** Accept, but strip the *learned* line before the deep-dive ships (the learned line is portfolio-altitude and the team manager cannot write it).

## Answer key

**Q1 — B.** The rollout-count scoreboard is the exact pattern — the count rises as rollouts accumulate regardless of whether the program is producing business value. Chapter 01 is explicit: it is "the portfolio-scale equivalent of '95% of licensed users logged in'" from mod-102. The remediation is an aggregated outcome metric with a confidence band in the headline; the rollout count moves to the operational appendix if it moves anywhere. (Chapter 01 — *Pattern 1 — the rollout-count scoreboard*.)

**Q2 — C.** Chapter 01's first aggregation discipline is "aggregate within category, not across." Time-per-task across drafting rollouts aggregates cleanly; across drafting *and* support-ticket workflows it is a category error because the workflows are different. The honest report is four-to-six category rollups; a grand total, if one appears, is a published weighted composite that Finance has an opinion on. A denominator or a confidence band on the wrong aggregation does not rescue it. (Chapter 01 — *Discipline 1 — aggregate within category, not across*.)

**Q3 — B.** Chapter 02 names the weekly rollup as an operating document for the enablement lead and the partners who allocate next week's work — explicitly *not* a VP-and-up artifact. The chapter is direct: "if a VP has asked to see it, the request is a signal that the monthly deep-dive is not landing and should be investigated separately." Recutting the rollup or trimming its misses section are both the wrong moves; the right move is to protect the cadence and open the deep-dive conversation. (Chapter 02 — *The weekly rollup → Purpose* and *What the weekly rollup is not*.)

**Q4 — C.** Chapter 02's misses-first discipline is explicit: every target that missed appears in Section 3 by name, with a reason, *before* any of the successes in Section 2 have been elaborated. The chapter directly addresses the "it sets the wrong tone" pushback — the alternative, all wins every quarter, has a short half-life, and program leaders who lead with misses teach executives that the hits are also real. The move is to decline, not to compromise with vendor benchmarks or a side memo. (Chapter 02 — *The misses-first discipline*.)

**Q5 — D.** Chapter 02 is direct: "The *learned* line is the most important sentence in the miss narrative. It is what distinguishes a program that is learning from a program that is rotating through failures." "We should have measured better" is a rollout-level non-commitment that functions as attribution-flight in miniature — Chapter 02 names it in the three miss-section failure modes. The credible *learned* line names a program-level change — a new rule for pilot design, a new interview question, a new attribution check. (Chapter 02 — *The miss narrative — a worked template* and *The misses-first discipline → Three failure modes to avoid*.)

**Q6 — D.** The opening carries two of Chapter 03's three theatre failure modes simultaneously. "78% of the workforce" is the *engagement lede* — a usage number as the lead, the adoption-percentage failure in executive-communication dress; the remediation is to open with an outcome against target. "Transformative milestone," "digital transformation journey" are the *vendor voice* — phrases indistinguishable from vendor marketing; the remediation is the organization's own language grounded in the organization's own measurements. Both patterns apply; choosing one misses the other. (Chapter 03 — *Pattern 1 — the engagement lede* and *Pattern 3 — the vendor voice*.)

**Q7 — B.** Chapter 03's *strategy abstraction* pattern is the direct diagnosis — the sentence makes no specific commitment the reader can act on, and over four quarters that abstraction erodes the writer's credibility to the point of program termination. The chapter's remediation is explicit: every executive artifact carries at least one specific, measurable commitment tied to a date — "In Q4, the retained-client drafting rollout will move from 11% to 22% …, reviewable at week 6." That is a commitment a reader can approve, challenge, or track. BLUF, vendor benchmarks, and appendix-move are not responses to the pattern. (Chapter 03 — *Pattern 2 — the strategy abstraction*.)

**Q8 — B.** Chapter 04's reading discipline is in its title: "method, not numbers." The Peng et al. 55.8% figure does not travel across task (one HTTP-server implementation vs. your drafting workflow), population (95 self-selected developers vs. your mandate cohort), cadence (short session vs. quarterly rollout), or baseline (study-instrumented vs. organizationally produced). Transplanting the headline figure is the vendor-number-headline failure from Chapter 01 in literature dress. The right move is to cite the Peng *method* — pre-registered primary outcome, telemetry-as-texture — in the methodology appendix, not the Peng number in the target. (Chapter 04 — *Why method travels and numbers don't* and *Peng et al.*.)

**Q9 — D.** Chapter 04's threat list names novelty effects directly: "Early adoption often shows exaggerated effects — operators are paying attention, measurement is tight, the tool is novel. The effect dampens at steady state." The useful response is Chapter 04 Pattern 3 — longitudinal observation, not cross-sectional — grounded in Brynjolfsson/Li/Raymond, whose single most-cited finding is that treatment effects *evolve* over tenure. The six-month measurement is more credible than the one-month. The other threats are real, but "will it hold up over time?" is specifically the novelty-effects question, not the selection, drift, or attribution question. (Chapter 04 — *The threats to validity the enablement lead carries → Novelty effects* and *Pattern 3 — longitudinal observation*.)

**Q10 — C.** Chapter 05's denominator has four components: tool licenses, enablement program spend, *embedded FTE time*, and *downstream support cost*. The invoice-plus-CoE-budget calculation captures the first two and omits the last two — which Chapter 05 names as "often the hardest to measure; always the first to be challenged as missing." The denominator discipline is the discipline of not making the denominator smaller than it is; the fully-loaded FTE multiplier is Finance's number, not the enablement lead's. Opportunity cost and pilot costs are explicitly *not* in the denominator. (Chapter 05 — *The denominator — cost → Four cost components* and *What does not belong in the denominator*.)

**Q11 — C.** Chapter 05 names this directly as the *reverse-engineered ROI* pattern: "The target ROI number is chosen first … and the attribution fraction or the denominator scope is adjusted until the number lands there. The methodology appendix is written to support the predetermined number, not to produce it. Watch for this pattern in your own drafts; the tell is that the attribution fraction changes between quarters without a corresponding change in the evidence that would justify it." The vignette matches the tell exactly: attribution fraction moves, evidence does not. Compounding-ROI-forever is a different pattern (projection, not attribution); a sensitivity analysis changes outputs under assumption variation, not inputs on authorial preference. (Chapter 05 — *Theatre to watch for in ROI reporting → The reverse-engineered ROI*.)

**Q12 — B.** Chapter 06's role-boundary section on the team manager names this exact pattern: "Public miss narratives in the exec scorecard. When a team's rollout misses, the miss narrative in the exec scorecard is the enablement lead's writing. The team manager is the source of the diagnosis; the lead is the author of the narrative. Team managers who are asked to write the exec-facing miss narrative are being asked to do executive communication without the skill or the sponsor relationship — a failure shape." The team manager supplies diagnosis and texture; the enablement lead writes the narrative. Routing to Finance or stripping the *learned* line both still break the role boundary. (Chapter 06 — *The interface with `ai-augmented-team-manager` → What the enablement lead does not ask the team manager to do*.)
