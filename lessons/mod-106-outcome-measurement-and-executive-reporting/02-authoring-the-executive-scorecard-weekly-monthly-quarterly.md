# Authoring the Executive Scorecard — Weekly, Monthly, Quarterly

> The scorecard is the recurring artifact the organization uses to decide whether the enablement program is working. It is not one document; it is three cadences — a weekly rollup, a monthly deep-dive, a quarterly board-ready review — each with a specific audience, a specific purpose, and a specific template. This chapter is the operating manual for all three. The non-obvious discipline is the one the role is most reluctant to adopt: *publish the targets that missed with the reason, not just the hits*. That is what separates a scorecard from a highlight reel.

## Three cadences, three audiences, three purposes

The temptation is to produce one scorecard and recut it for each audience. Resist it. The three cadences serve three different decisions, and the formats differ accordingly.

| Cadence | Audience | Purpose | Length | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly rollup | Enablement lead, direct partners (governance, L&D, analyst, team managers) | Operate the program — surface this week's drift, allocate next week's attention | One page | Enablement lead |
| Monthly deep-dive | Exec sponsor (CAIO / CIO / CTO), Finance partner, head of governance, functional VPs with live rollouts | Hold the program to its quarterly targets; surface investment asks | Three to five pages | Enablement lead, co-signed by exec sponsor for distribution |
| Quarterly board-ready review | Board (or exec-leadership-equivalent), CEO, CFO, CAIO, head of governance | Shape the next quarter's investment, resource allocation, and strategic direction | Six to ten pages plus appendix | Co-authored with exec sponsor; sign-off from CAIO before the board packet lands |

Each cadence consumes the outputs of the one below it. The weekly rollup is the raw material for the monthly; the monthly deep-dives for the quarter are the raw material for the quarterly review. Do not attempt to generate the quarterly review by writing it from scratch — the honesty of the quarterly review is a function of how well the weekly rollups caught the signal in real time.

## The weekly rollup

### Purpose

The weekly rollup is an operating document. It is read by the enablement lead and the small set of partners who allocate next week's work against this week's signal. It is not for executives; if a VP has asked to see it, the request is a signal that the monthly deep-dive is not landing and should be investigated separately.

### Structure

One page. Five sections, fixed order. Never rearrange — the order is part of the discipline.

**1. Outcome movements this week.** For each rollout on the live roster, one line: the outcome metric, the directional move (↑/↓/flat), the baseline, the current value. No commentary; the metric speaks or it does not. If an outcome metric was not updated this week (the measurement cycle is monthly, say), state that explicitly — *outcome refreshed monthly; next update DD-MM*.

**2. Workflow-interview signals this week.** Three to five bullets, each an anonymized quote or pattern from the week's interviews that cuts against or sharpens the aggregate numbers. If no interviews ran this week, the bullet count is zero — write "no interviews this cycle" rather than padding.

**3. Usage telemetry — floor check.** One line per rollout: *active-use curve healthy / flat / declining*. No percentages, no vanity numbers. The weekly rollup uses telemetry only as a sanity check that the tool is alive.

**4. Risks and misses.** Every outcome metric below its target *this week* is listed by name with a one-sentence reason. "Legal drafting rollout — median time-to-first-draft flat versus baseline; investigation ongoing, suspected workflow-fit issue in cross-border matters." The temptation is to omit the miss "because it will resolve next week"; the discipline is to list it anyway.

**5. Next week's attention allocation.** Three bullets at most. Which rollouts, interviews, or investigations are on the front burner for the following week, and why. This is the section that converts the rollup into operating work.

### What the weekly rollup is not

- It is not a status report. "Continuing to roll out X" is not a sentence on this page.
- It is not a storytelling vehicle. Keep it dense; the storytelling belongs in the monthly deep-dive.
- It is not shared outside the operating circle. Monthly deep-dives are the VP-and-up artifact.

## The monthly deep-dive

### Purpose

The monthly deep-dive is the first surface where executives hear about the program on a predictable cadence. Its job is to hold the program accountable to its *quarterly* targets, flag investment asks that cannot wait for the quarterly review, and surface the risks that have the shape of future scorecard misses.

### Structure

Three to five pages, with a fixed section order. Numbered so reviewers can cite "section 4 of this month's deep-dive" without ambiguity.

**1. One-paragraph executive summary.** Bottom-line-up-front: what moved, what missed, what the asks are. Written last. Three to five sentences. See [Chapter 03](03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md) for the BLUF discipline.

**2. Outcome metrics against quarterly target.** For each live rollout: the outcome metric, the quarterly target, this month's value, the gap, the directional trajectory. Report in categories — drafting workflows, summarization workflows, support workflows, etc. — not as a single list of twenty rollouts.

**3. Misses and their reasons.** A named section. One sub-heading per missed metric; two to three paragraphs on each, covering: what the target was, what the actual was, what the diagnosis is (workflow-fit? measurement issue? change-management lag? vendor regression?), and what is being done. If a miss has not been diagnosed, say so — "diagnosis in progress" is a legitimate answer with a date attached.

**4. Qualitative ground truth.** Two or three pattern findings from the month's workflow interviews, each with one anonymized quote. The patterns are what the dashboard can't see; the quotes are what makes the finding land.

**5. Risks on the one-to-three-month horizon.** Three to five named risks with brief descriptions, impact assessments, and ownership. These are the signals that have not yet produced scorecard misses but are likely to.

**6. Investment asks and decisions requested.** Explicit list. Each ask has a specific decision requested, a decision owner, and a decision-by date. If there are no asks this month, state that. "Ask for decision" without a decision owner is a missed ask; put the name on the ask.

**7. Status on last month's decisions.** What was asked last month, what was decided, where the work stands. This section is the mechanism that keeps the monthly cadence honest — an ask from last month that is not closed or re-raised this month is the ask falling through the floor.

### The misses-first discipline

A monthly deep-dive without a misses section — or with a misses section populated with euphemisms — is not a scorecard. The role's credibility with executives is built on the *second* month, not the first: executives who read a program's deep-dive believe the hits if the misses were credible last month.

The rule: every target that missed appears in section 3 by name, with a reason, before any of the successes in section 2 have been elaborated. The order is deliberate. Program leaders who lead with misses teach executives that the hits are also real; program leaders who bury misses teach executives to discount the hits.

Three failure modes to avoid in the misses section:

- **Euphemism.** "The rollout faced some headwinds." The metric missed by X%. State it.
- **Scope-swap.** "We decided to re-target against a different metric." Acknowledged in section 3 is an acceptable move; sweeping into section 2 as a success is not.
- **Attribution flight.** "The miss was caused by the business unit's lack of readiness." Sometimes true; usually dodging. The role owns the miss on the scorecard and investigates the BU separately.

## The quarterly board-ready review

### Purpose

The quarterly review is a board-facing or exec-leadership-facing artifact. Its job is to shape the next quarter's investment and the next year's strategic direction. The audience reads deep programs but does not have time for operational texture; the review distills twelve weeks of program activity into a decision-ready narrative.

### Structure

Six to ten pages, with a long appendix. Eight numbered sections:

**1. One-page executive summary.** The first page the board reads. Most will not read past it. Treat it as the decision artifact. See [Chapter 03](03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md) for the structure.

**2. Portfolio-level outcome rollups.** The category rollups from [Chapter 01](01-the-measurement-stack-at-org-scope.md) with confidence bands. One chart per outcome family. The numbers on the chart are measured on the organization's cohort — no vendor numbers.

**3. The hit list.** Three to five rollouts, by name, that moved their outcome metric against target. Each gets a brief — what the rollout was, what the outcome moved, why it moved (workflow-fit, change-management quality, training depth), and what is replicable elsewhere in the portfolio. The *why* is what the board uses to allocate next quarter.

**4. The miss list — and the quarter's lessons.** Equal in weight to section 3. Three to five rollouts that missed targets. Each gets a brief with the same structure: what the rollout was, what missed, why, what has been changed or will be. The section ends with two to four sentences on *lessons learned at the program level* — patterns across the misses that are shaping how next quarter's rollouts will be run.

**5. Qualitative ground truth, at quarterly resolution.** Two or three pattern findings from the quarter's interviews. These are not operational anecdotes; they are findings large enough to shape next-quarter investment.

**6. ROI narrative.** The value-realised story for the quarter, composed with Finance per [Chapter 05](05-partnering-with-finance-on-cost-model-and-roi.md). Numerator, denominator, attribution logic, confidence band. One chart, one paragraph, one methodology footnote.

**7. Strategic risks on the one-to-three-quarter horizon.** Three to five named risks: vendor concentration, regulatory shifts (AI Act, sector regulators), capability gaps in the champion network or training curriculum, dependency risks on specific internal builds, workforce-impact risks that have surfaced in interviews but not in the dashboard. Each with ownership and a monitoring plan.

**8. Investment asks and strategic decisions requested.** The explicit list. Budget, headcount, decision-rights changes, scope expansions or contractions. Each with the structure from the monthly: decision owner, decision-by date, cost and value framing.

### Appendix

The appendix carries the per-rollout detail that would overweight the narrative sections — the full rollout roster with status, the full interview-coverage matrix, the methodology notes for the outcome metrics and the ROI calculation, and the delta against last quarter's commitments. Board members who read the appendix are checking the work; the appendix is what makes the narrative sections defensible on challenge.

## The miss narrative — a worked template

Every missed target gets the same five-part narrative in the deep-dive and the quarterly. Keep it short — three to five sentences per miss — but non-negotiable in shape.

- **What the target was.** From the pilot charter or from the quarterly target-setting cycle. Quote it as stated at the time.
- **What the actual was.** The current value, measured on the organization's cohort. Include the denominator.
- **Why it missed.** One of a small set of diagnoses: workflow-fit issue, measurement issue, change-management lag, vendor regression, cohort-shift, dependency miss. Pick one as dominant; multi-cause misses get a dominant diagnosis plus a secondary note.
- **What is being done.** The specific intervention, the owner, the next review date. If the intervention is "investigating," say so with a date attached.
- **What was learned.** What this miss teaches at the program level — not at the rollout level. The program-level lesson is what justifies publishing the miss; without it, the miss is a footnote.

Worked example (fictional):

> **Miss: Legal drafting rollout, Q3 target of 30% reduction in time-to-first-draft, actual 11% reduction.**
>
> *Target.* 30% reduction in median time-to-first-draft on comparable cross-border advisory matters, measured on the pilot cohort of 180 associates, versus the pre-rollout baseline of 4.2 hours.
>
> *Actual.* 11% reduction (median 3.7 hours, n=174 associates over 12 weeks). Wide variance by matter type; retained-client advisory saw 24% reduction, new-matter drafting saw 2%.
>
> *Why.* Workflow-fit issue on new-matter drafting; the tool's context-loading step is too light for first-time matter research. Confirmed in 6 of 8 interviews with senior associates in the new-matter cohort.
>
> *What is being done.* Re-scoped the drafting rollout to retained-client advisory only for Q4; opened a Stage 2 selection re-examination (see [mod-102 Chapter 01](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/01-the-three-stage-selection-decision.md)) for new-matter drafting; owner is J. Rivera, next review 15 October.
>
> *Learned.* Matter-type heterogeneity inside a single legal workflow is large enough to need separate pilots. Program-wide: single-cohort drafting pilots in professional-services firms will be sub-decomposed by work type before rollout from this quarter forward.

The learned line is the most important sentence in the miss narrative. It is what distinguishes a program that is learning from a program that is rotating through failures.

## The annotations that keep the scorecard auditable

Three annotations belong on every cadence of the scorecard. They are the artifacts that let a six-months-later auditor, Finance partner, or new exec sponsor reconstruct what you were reporting and why.

- **Metric definitions.** For every outcome metric, a one-paragraph definition — what is measured, on which cohort, at which cadence, with what denominator. A metric that cannot be defined in a paragraph is not a scorecard metric.
- **Baseline provenance.** For every outcome metric, where the pre-rollout baseline came from — the pilot charter, a prior measurement, a sampled ground-truth exercise. Baselines without provenance erode fast; a published baseline with provenance survives staffing changes.
- **Confidence framing.** Every portfolio-level number appears with a confidence band and the methodology footnote that produced it. "22% (90% CI: 14%–29%, bootstrap over operator-level deltas)" rather than "22%."

These three annotations usually live in an appendix section, not in the narrative. They are what makes the narrative defensible under challenge.

## Summary

- Three cadences — weekly rollup, monthly deep-dive, quarterly board-ready review — each with a specific audience, purpose, and template. Do not collapse them into one artifact.
- The weekly rollup is one page, five fixed sections, read by the operating circle only.
- The monthly deep-dive is three to five pages, seven fixed sections, with a named *misses* section before any success narrative is elaborated.
- The quarterly board-ready review is six to ten pages plus appendix, eight sections, with an equal-weight hit list and miss list.
- The miss narrative is non-negotiable in shape: target, actual, why, what is being done, what was learned. The *learned* line is the most important sentence.
- Metric definitions, baseline provenance, and confidence framing are the three annotations that keep the scorecard auditable six months later; they live in the appendix and are the quiet infrastructure of the role's credibility.
