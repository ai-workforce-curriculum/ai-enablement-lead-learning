# The Exec-Communication Story That Ties the Track Together

> You have spent eleven modules building surfaces — the selection framework, the policy stack, the training curriculum, the champion network, the scorecard, the budget, the industry flavour, the incident path, the commissioning brief, the CoE operating loop, the credentials portfolio. A CAIO who sits down with you for sixty minutes is not going to read eleven documents. They are going to ask you one question: *what does your program do for this organisation, and what should I do about it.* This chapter is the narrative that answers the question — not a tour of the surfaces, but the story that makes the surfaces add up to something a board or a chief AI officer will act on.

## Why the story is the deliverable

The exec-communication craft from [mod-106 Chapter 03](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md) authored the three artifact shapes — the board update, the LT briefing, the strategic memo. Each of those lands in a standing cadence against a specific decision. The story this chapter authors is different. It is the running narrative the role carries into every one of those artifacts and every corridor conversation with the sponsor, the CAIO, the board chair, the recruiter, the hiring exec at the next step. It is the version of the program that fits in a single breath and still earns a second meeting.

The story has to carry three loads at once:

1. **Legibility to the exec audience.** The CAIO reads at CAIO altitude, not at CoE-operating altitude. The story has to land without the reader having to translate from "service catalog slot 4" into "the thing that moves the number the board cares about."
2. **Fidelity to the operating evidence.** The story cannot invent a narrative that the scorecard, decision log, and incident register will not sustain under a pointed question. The misses-first discipline from [mod-106 Chapter 02](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md) is load-bearing here.
3. **Positioning for the next step on the ladder.** The same story is what the next-step hiring exec will read on the CV, in the conference talk, in the LinkedIn posts. It has to be the enablement program's narrative *and* the lead's professional narrative at the same time — not two stories told from different pages.

A lead whose scorecard is impeccable and whose story is incoherent loses the room. A lead whose story is coherent and whose evidence does not stand up loses the room the second time. Both halves are the job.

## The eleven surfaces, named at story altitude

Before the story can be told it has to be collected. Each prior module contributed a surface; at story altitude each one reduces to a single sentence of what it proved:

- **[mod-102](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/README.md) — selection and rollout.** *We know how to choose a tool, run a bounded pilot against a baseline, and ship the artefact bundle that makes the tool safe to open.* Evidence surface: the three-stage decision records, the pilot adopt/extend/kill decisions, the rollout artefact bundles that shipped.
- **[mod-103](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/README.md) — policy co-authoring.** *We author policy as redline-ready drafts with governance, not as hand-offs to legal.* Evidence surface: the policy stack, the redline log, the governance council attendance record.
- **[mod-104](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/README.md) — training and literacy.** *We have moved the organisation from aware to fluent on measurable targets, with role-based playbooks underneath.* Evidence surface: the fluency assessment series, the Article 4 literacy evidence trail, the role-based playbook library.
- **[mod-105](../mod-105-champion-networks-and-change-management/README.md) — champions and change.** *We run a champion network as a two-way feedback loop, not as a newsletter list.* Evidence surface: the field-signal pipeline, the four-phase lifecycle records, the resistance-pattern interventions and outcomes.
- **[mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md) — measurement and exec reporting.** *We publish honest numbers with confidence bands, with the misses in the lead.* Evidence surface: the weekly scorecard, the monthly deep-dive, the quarterly board-ready review.
- **[mod-107](../mod-107-budget-roi-and-build-vs-buy-at-org-scope/README.md) — budget and build-vs-buy.** *We can defend every dollar of the program against a four-column build-vs-buy matrix and a cost model that maps to the catalog.* Evidence surface: the cost model, the ROI confidence-band track record, the quarterly build-vs-buy re-read.
- **[mod-108](../mod-108-industry-and-regulatory-flavours-of-enablement/README.md) — industry flavour.** *We adjust the operating model to the industry's regulatory shape without becoming legal counsel.* Evidence surface: the industry-adjusted policy stack, the regulator-facing evidence trails where applicable.
- **[mod-109](../mod-109-ai-incident-response-and-enablement-side-rollback/README.md) — incident and rollback.** *We have run, or are ready to run, the enablement-side incident path — kill switch, communication, evidence, governance handoff.* Evidence surface: the incident post-mortems, the rollback drill records, the policy / training / rollout changes folded back from incidents.
- **[mod-110](../mod-110-commissioning-internal-ai-tools-and-custom-agents/README.md) — commissioning.** *We commission internal builds against a brief, not against enthusiasm; we say no when the brief does not hold.* Evidence surface: the commissioning briefs shipped, the inherited-prototype assessments, the honest no-go calls.
- **[mod-111](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/README.md) — CoE operations.** *We run the operating loop and the quarterly ritual at a cadence the exec audience can read without translation.* Evidence surface: the service catalog, the three operational instruments, the vendor portfolio, the quarterly ritual record, the community of practice.
- **[mod-111 Chapter 07](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/07-the-credentials-portfolio-as-shared-vocabulary.md) — credentials portfolio.** *We hold the shared vocabulary that lets the cross-functional surfaces work without the lead being the only translator.* Evidence surface: the portfolio holdings, the places each vocabulary is read in the operating ritual.

These eleven one-sentence claims are the raw material. The story the chapter builds is what welds them together.

## The shape of the story — four moves, no more

A story that keeps the exec audience in the room has four moves in sequence. Any more and the audience disengages; any fewer and the story is missing a load-bearing section. The four moves are not slides; they are the scaffolding of whatever artefact the story is landing in — the one-pager, the fifteen-minute talk, the five-minute LT briefing opener.

### Move 1 — the organisational problem statement in one sentence

The story opens with the problem the enablement program exists to solve, stated at the altitude the audience is thinking at. Not "we needed an AI CoE." Not "AI adoption is accelerating." The sentence names the organisation's specific exposure that the program closes.

Three illustrative opens, calibrated by audience:

- *For a 12,000-person bank CAIO:* "Our retail and commercial lending operators are using AI tooling in ways our policy stack had not caught up to, and SR 11-7 was coming to AI."
- *For a 4,000-person consultancy board:* "Our billable practices were adopting AI at project level without a story we could tell to a prospective client about how their data would be handled."
- *For an 8,000-person regional health system LT:* "Our clinical teams were asking for AI-assisted documentation and our HIPAA posture had no answer that was faster than 'no.'"

The sentence does three things. It names a specific risk or opportunity (not "AI generally"). It names the organisational unit the risk lives in (not "the organisation"). It names what would have happened without intervention (not "we needed to do something"). If the sentence does not do all three, the story will drift into generality in the next move.

### Move 2 — what the program did, named in three evidence-backed claims

The eleven surfaces above are too many to carry into the story. The second move reduces them to three claims, each backed by one specific evidence surface. The three-claim shape is the discipline that keeps the story at exec altitude; four claims makes it a tour, two claims makes it look selective.

Which three depend on the organisation and the audience. The patterns:

- **Bank or finance CAIO audience:** policy + measurement + incident. The CAIO's exposure sits in all three; the three-claim shape reads as "we are clean."
- **Consultancy or professional-services board:** selection + training + commissioning. The board's exposure sits in client-facing capability; the shape reads as "we can sell what we built."
- **Health system LT:** policy + training + incident. The LT's exposure sits in clinical-safety and regulatory cover; the shape reads as "we are not the hospital's next recall."
- **Ed-tech or tech-vendor board:** rollout + commissioning + ROI. The board's exposure sits in product velocity and investor narrative; the shape reads as "the AI investment thesis is real and measured."

Each claim in the three is written to the three-sentence BLUF shape from [mod-106 Chapter 03](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md) — the finding, the specific evidence surface, the confidence band or caveat.

### Move 3 — the misses, named without theatre

The story has to include what the program has missed or is still missing. The misses-first discipline from [mod-106 Chapter 02](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/02-authoring-the-executive-scorecard-weekly-monthly-quarterly.md) applies at story altitude too: the misses do not appear in a footnote on page seven; they appear in move three of a four-move story, on their own ground, named flatly.

What belongs in move three:

- **One target that missed in the last cycle.** Named with the actual delta, not softened. Named with the diagnosis — specific enough that the audience can tell diagnosis from excuse.
- **One capability the program does not yet have.** Named as a gap, not as a "roadmap item." The audience can tell the difference.
- **One risk the program is carrying knowingly.** Named with the mitigation posture — accepted, under review, being escalated. Not "we are monitoring this" without a next action.

The move does not dominate the story. Three sentences on each, at most. The point is not to elaborate the misses; the point is to put them in the open before the audience finds them for themselves. A story that credibly carries its misses in move three earns the audience's attention through move four. A story that hides them loses the room when a sharp question in Q&A exposes one.

### Move 4 — the ask, named with the confidence

The story closes with what the program needs next. This is the move most enablement leads short-change — they end the story at the evidence and leave the ask implicit. The implicit ask is the ask the audience does not act on.

Three shapes of ask the move can carry, in decreasing order of commitment:

- **A decision.** "Approve the Q1 budget line for the embedded-partner expansion to the retail BU." A specific action, with a specific amount or scope, decidable in the room or in the two-week window after it.
- **An alignment.** "Align on the enablement program taking the enablement-side incident path for the clinical-documentation rollout, with governance co-signing the brief." A specific commitment to a cross-functional posture, agreed in the meeting, documented in the follow-up.
- **An awareness.** "I want the board to know that if the EU AI Act enforcement cases trend in the direction the current filings suggest, our EEA-touching flow will need policy and training updates inside Q2." A specific heads-up on a specific horizon, so the audience is not surprised later.

Every story closes on exactly one of the three. The confidence attaches to the ask — "high confidence this is the right ask, medium confidence on the sizing" — and the move names the decision date so the ask does not slide into a standing item no one acts on.

## The story at three artefact lengths

The same story ships at three different lengths depending on the surface. Treat these as the same story at different compressions — not three stories with a shared theme.

### The thirty-second version — the elevator

Thirty seconds is one sentence per move; sixty words total. The version the lead carries into a corridor conversation with the CAIO or into the opening of an interview round.

Worked example (bank, consultancy, or health-system shape):

> *Our [BU] was using AI in ways our [policy/HIPAA/SR 11-7] stack had not caught up to. We closed the gap with a [three-claim shape]; we are still missing [one gap]; and I am asking [the specific ask] at [the specific decision point].*

The elevator version is the test case for the longer versions. If the three-claim shape, the one gap, and the one ask cannot fit in sixty coherent words, the longer versions will drift.

### The one-page version — the memo

One page is the shape the LT briefing opener, the board-update appendix lead, and the CV summary section all share. Approximately 400 words, formatted as four short paragraphs matching the four moves. The BLUF discipline from [mod-106 Chapter 03](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md) governs the paragraph structure.

The one-page version is where the confidence bands appear. "22% reduction, 90% CI 14–29%, pilot cohort n=180" is the shape the credibility hangs on. The elevator version carries the point estimate; the memo version carries the band and the sample size.

### The fifteen-minute version — the talk

Fifteen minutes is the shape the conference talk ([Chapter 05](05-the-public-artifact-set-conference-talk-writing-open-source.md)), the quarterly board review, and the next-step interview panel all share. The four moves occupy the fifteen minutes in roughly equal parts: three to four minutes per move, with Q&A folded into or after move four.

The talk-length version includes one worked example per claim — not three, not a parade of logos. Executives read repetition as stretching. The talk version also includes the "what we tried that did not work" surface, which is where move three expands from the three-sentence memo version into a two-minute honest walk through one correction the program made and what the correction cost.

## How the story holds up under hostile reading

The story will be read hostilely at some point. A board member who is sceptical of AI investment; a recruiter who is screening against three other candidates at the same level; a next-step hiring exec who is comparing the lead's narrative to the one the role already has in-house. Three failure modes to pre-empt:

### Failure mode 1 — the story does not match the evidence a cold reader can verify

The hostile reader will ask to see the scorecard, the incident register, or the vendor portfolio. If the story's three claims do not reconcile to the artefacts in minutes, the credibility collapses. The correction is not to soften the story; it is to ensure the story is told *from* the artefacts, not around them. Every claim in move two points to a specific document the reader can be shown.

### Failure mode 2 — the misses in move three are either too soft or too dramatic

Too-soft misses read as a program hiding something; too-dramatic misses read as a program flailing. The calibration is the three-sentence shape above: the delta is stated flatly, the diagnosis names a specific mechanism, the posture is specific (accepted / under review / escalated). If the lead cannot name the specific mechanism of a miss, the diagnosis is not yet real and the miss is not yet ready for move three — do more work, then tell the story.

### Failure mode 3 — the ask in move four is a wish list, not a decision

"We would love more headcount and more budget and more sponsor time" is not an ask; it is a wish. The ask is one decision, at one named point, with one owner. If move four is multiple asks or a bundle, the hostile reader hears "they do not know what they most need next," and the narrative reads as unprioritised. Pick the one ask that unlocks the next quarter; put the others in the quarterly review's "future asks" section where they belong.

## The story and the next step on the ladder

The story is one artefact; it carries two loads. For the organisation, it is the running narrative that keeps the program funded and credible. For the lead, it is the professional narrative that positions the next step. [Chapter 02](02-choosing-the-next-step-on-the-ladder.md) picks up the second load specifically — how the same four-move story is shaded differently depending on whether the next step is `head-of-ai-governance` (governance program leadership), `senior-ai-governance-architect` (control-library architecture), `chief-ai-officer` (executive scope), or `ai-engineering-manager` (engineering-adjacent people management).

## Summary

- **The exec-communication story is the single narrative that ties the eleven prior modules into something a CAIO or a board will act on.** It is the deliverable, not a by-product.
- **The story carries three loads at once:** legibility to the exec audience, fidelity to the operating evidence, and positioning for the next step on the ladder. All three have to be true at the same time.
- **The shape is four moves:** the organisational problem in one sentence; three evidence-backed claims; the misses named without theatre; the ask named with its confidence. Any more and the audience disengages; any fewer and the story is incomplete.
- **The same story ships at three lengths** — elevator (sixty words), memo (one page), talk (fifteen minutes) — with the same structure at each compression. The elevator version is the test case for the longer versions.
- **Three hostile-reading failure modes to pre-empt:** the story not reconciling to the artefacts, the misses being too soft or too dramatic, and the ask being a wish list rather than a single decidable action.
- **The story carries into the next-step-on-the-ladder positioning** that the remaining chapters of this module pick up. One narrative, two loads — the organisation's and the lead's.
