# The Decision Points That Separate This Role from Adjacent Tracks

> [Chapter 02](02-choosing-the-next-step-on-the-ladder.md) chose a path in advance; chapters 03–05 built toward it. The step itself happens at a moment — a quarter, sometimes a specific week — when the current role's work has saturated and the next role's work is the honest answer to the lead's energy and the organisation's shape. This chapter names the signals that identify that moment for each of the four paths. Specifically: when enablement scope has saturated and governance program leadership begins; when CoE ops has saturated and CAIO-level strategy begins; and the parallel moments for the architect and engineering-manager paths. The goal is to recognise the transition when it happens, not to force it on a calendar.

## Why the transition point is a decision, not an event

The step from level 45 to the next level does not happen automatically. Many enablement leads reach steady state, run the operating loop well for three to five years, and never step up — not because the step is unavailable but because the signals were ambiguous and the lead deferred the decision one quarter at a time. The most common trajectory for a lead who cannot name the transition signals is a long plateau at level 45, usually ended by an external approach that happened to arrive at the right moment.

The transition signals this chapter authors are the lead's internal instrument — the set of observations that, when they co-occur, mean the current role has saturated against the lead's growth. The signals are specific; they are not "I feel ready" (which is usually wrong) and they are not "the opportunity exists" (which is usually true and usually not actionable). They are the operating evidence that the role has stopped being the place the lead grows.

Three guardrails before the signals:

- **The signals come from the current role's evidence, not from the next role's attractiveness.** A lead who orients to the next role because the next role sounds better is usually not ready. A lead who orients because the current role has stopped being the growth site is usually ready.
- **No single signal is sufficient.** Each of the sections below names three to four signals; the transition is when three of them have co-occurred over two to three quarters. One signal in a quarter is noise.
- **The signals are read against the lead's own career, not against the organisation's needs.** The organisation may want the lead to stay at level 45 forever because the current role is funded and stable; that is not a transition signal, that is an institutional preference. The lead's career is the frame.

## When enablement scope has saturated and governance program leadership begins

The transition from `ai-enablement-lead` (level 45) to `head-of-ai-governance` (level 60) is not a promotion inside the same job; it is a crossing to the Governance family where the program becomes the surface the lead owns end-to-end. Specific signals that the crossing is at hand:

### Signal 1 — the lead's weekly surface is majority-governance-side

The three questions from [mod-111 Chapter 01](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/01-the-coe-at-steady-state.md) (what is in flight, blocked, at risk) stop being majority enablement-operating questions. The lead's Thursday writing is increasingly policy-stack redlines, standards-mapping reviews, regulator-touching evidence packages, and council-agenda authoring — not catalog throughput, training-curriculum revisions, or champion-community health reviews. The lead has been running the governance partnership so deeply that the governance work is the main work, and the enablement work has become the undercurrent.

When this signal arrives, the lead is doing the head-of-governance job at level-45 title. The step is to formalise what is already happening.

### Signal 2 — the governance counterpart treats the lead as a peer, not a partner

The `head-of-ai-governance` the lead has co-authored with for two years starts bringing the lead into conversations that are governance-program-level, not enablement-side. The regulator meetings; the board's AI risk committee pre-reads; the control-library architectural reviews with the senior architect. The lead is being briefed as a governance insider, not consulted as an enablement outsider.

When this signal arrives, the governance counterpart has likely already decided to sponsor the step. The next conversation with them is the one where the step comes up explicitly.

### Signal 3 — the enablement work has a credible successor

A lead who could not leave their enablement seat because no one else could run the service catalog, the quarterly ritual, and the champion community is a lead whose transition will delay. The signal that the step is at hand is the opposite: an operations analyst, an embedded enablement partner, or an L&D counterpart has grown into the shape where they could take the lead's role with a transition plan. The role is survivable in the lead's absence.

This signal is the lead's own succession work paying off. A lead who has not invested in succession will not see this signal; the transition will stall because the lead cannot in good faith leave.

### Signal 4 — the enablement scorecard is boring

The scorecard runs. The numbers move in the expected directions. The quarterly miss narrative is credible and the quarterly ask is predictable. The lead can draft the board update in an hour because the shape is now familiar. The challenge has moved from "will the program land" to "how do I keep the program running at the altitude it has reached."

When this signal arrives, the operating challenge has become operational, not developmental. The lead's growth is now coming from the governance work — which is the transition signal.

### The counter-signals for this path

The transition is not at hand if any of the following apply:

- A major enablement surface is in a known-fragile state (a new rollout in its first pilot, an incident open, a vendor transition mid-flight). The lead's responsibility is to stabilise before stepping up.
- The governance counterpart has not sponsored the step — if the counterpart is unavailable, uninterested, or opposed, the internal transition is not available and the lead is looking at an external search, which has a different preparation window.
- The lead's Chapter 04 credentials positioning has not yet caught up to the path — no Lead Implementer, no AIGP at exam level, no standards-reading brief delivered to the council. Step the preparation forward before stepping the role forward.

## When CoE ops has saturated and CAIO-level strategy begins

The transition from `ai-enablement-lead` (level 45) to `chief-ai-officer` (level 70) is a 25-level step across the Governance family. It is rare as a direct move; more common is the two-step trajectory through `head-of-ai-governance` (60) or through a lateral into CIO / CDO deputy roles. The direct step is possible but it happens inside specific organisational conditions:

### Signal 1 — the CAIO seat is vacant or newly created, and the sponsor is advocating

The organisation is standing up a CAIO function for the first time, or the sitting CAIO has moved and the seat is being re-staffed. The lead's sponsor (often the CEO, the COO, the board chair's AI committee lead) has raised the possibility. Specifically — the lead has not raised it; the sponsor has. The signal depends on this directionality. A lead advocating themselves into a CAIO seat the sponsor has not opened is a lead whose signal is misread.

### Signal 2 — the lead has been running the exec-communication story long enough that the board reads it

The lead's board updates have been authored, not drafted, for four or more consecutive quarters. The board has specifically commented on the quality of the narrative, the honesty of the misses, the discipline of the asks. The board chair (or the chair of the AI committee) has directly complimented or questioned the lead on the record. The audience the CAIO writes to is the audience the lead is already writing to.

### Signal 3 — the organisation's enablement work has become one of several AI surfaces, not the primary one

The organisation has matured past the "enablement is where AI lives" phase. Product AI, platform AI, M&A-driven AI, revenue-side AI — several of these are now live, each with its own leader, and the enablement function has become one voice among several at the AI strategy table. The CAIO job is the role that integrates these; the enablement lead sees that integration need and recognises it as unclaimed.

### Signal 4 — the lead's own thesis has become enterprise-level, not operating-level

The lead's own talk / writing / open-source artefacts ([Chapter 05](05-the-public-artifact-set-conference-talk-writing-open-source.md)) have shifted from enablement-operating altitude ("here is how to run a CoE") to enterprise-AI altitude ("here is how an organisation reorganises for AI-era operating leverage"). The thesis the lead carries in their own voice has outgrown the enablement operating loop; the step into the CAIO seat is the step where the voice fits the role.

### The counter-signals for this path

- The sponsor advocating the step is not the CEO or a board member — if a peer VP or the CIO is advocating but the CEO is not, the step is to the deputy role, not to the CAIO seat.
- The lead has not yet produced a P&L-adjacent artefact (the quantified portfolio-level ROI, the business-case-grade budget defence, the cost-recovery proposal). The CAIO conversation reads P&L fluency; a lead without evidence of it will be redirected.
- The organisation's AI work is still majority-enablement; the CAIO role is premature, and the lead should run `head-of-ai-governance` (level 60) as the intermediate step.

## When the architectural surface has saturated and control-library architecture begins

The transition from `ai-enablement-lead` (45) to `senior-ai-governance-architect` (50) is a sideways-with-step move at approximately the same altitude but in a different discipline. The signals are more specific because the step is less common:

### Signal 1 — the lead is being asked to produce the taxonomy, not just use it

The organisation's control library has had a de facto owner (often a senior analyst, sometimes the `head-of-ai-governance` directly). That owner has started asking the lead to co-author the taxonomy's shape — not to apply it, but to decide it. The lead is reading ISO/IEC 42001 clauses and NIST AI RMF categories not as background but as the direct source material for structural decisions.

When this signal arrives, the lead is doing architect work at enablement-lead title. The step is to formalise it.

### Signal 2 — the applied- and risk-engineer peer surfaces have deepened into architectural conversation

The commissioning work ([mod-110](../mod-110-commissioning-internal-ai-tools-and-custom-agents/README.md)) and the `ai-risk-engineer` partnership ([mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-role-and-the-org-wide-enablement-operating-model/04-handoffs-with-adjacent-roles.md)) have moved from service-request shape to architectural-dialogue shape. The lead is in the architecture review meetings; the architecture decision records cite the lead's input; the red-team-to-control translation is a conversation the lead participates in at depth.

### Signal 3 — the lead is reading standards documents for structure, not for compliance

A lead running enablement reads ISO/IEC 42001 to know what the governance side cares about. A lead approaching the architect transition reads the same document for the structural decisions it contains — which clauses are prescriptive and which are suggestive, how Annex A's control categories relate to ISO/IEC 27001's, where the standard's language carries ambiguity the implementer resolves. The read is architectural, not operational.

### The counter-signals for this path

- The lead has not held `Lead Auditor` credentials or shipped an ADR at architectural altitude. The architect transition requires evidence the enablement work does not naturally produce; the preparation gap is still open.
- The organisation does not have a senior architect role at level 50 — the control-library work is done inside the head-of-governance's team, and the step is to that role instead.
- The lead's energy is still in the operating-ritual shape, not in the architectural-decision shape. The Chapter 02 factor 1 read should have caught this; if it did not, the transition conversation will.

## When the commissioning surface has saturated and engineering management begins

The transition from `ai-enablement-lead` (45) to `ai-engineering-manager` (50) is a sideways-with-step across to the Engineering family. The signals are different from the governance-side transitions:

### Signal 1 — the applied- / agentic-engineer peers have become the lead's primary partner surface

The commissioning work is deep enough that the applied-ai-engineer and agentic-ai-engineer peers at level 30 are now the lead's primary partners — not the governance counterpart, not the L&D counterpart, not the CAIO. The weekly operating surface is majority engineering-partnership; the standing one-on-ones are with engineering ICs and tech leads.

### Signal 2 — the lead is reviewing engineering decisions, not just briefs

The commissioning conversations have moved past brief-and-acceptance into architecture-and-code-review participation. The lead is in the architecture review meetings at depth, has an opinion on the evaluation harness, participates in the deployment pipeline decisions. The brief is no longer where the lead's engagement starts and ends.

### Signal 3 — the engineering-manager counterpart has signalled sponsorship

The sitting engineering manager (level 40–50 in the AI Engineering family) has specifically commented on the lead's engineering adjacency. "You could run this team" is an informal signal; "we have an opening and I think you should think about it" is the specific signal. The signal typically comes from the engineering manager the lead has partnered with most closely, not from a stranger in the engineering org.

### Signal 4 — the lead has done the engineering-fluency work in [Chapter 03](03-the-deliberate-development-plan-toward-the-chosen-next-step.md)

The engineering-fluency artefact has shipped — the production-quality open-source engineering tool, the code the lead has contributed, the hands-on commissioning that went deeper than brief-to-acceptance. The signal is that the artefact is in the world and the engineering audience reads it as credible, not that the lead is planning to produce it.

### The counter-signals for this path

- The lead has not shipped an engineering artefact that reads as credible to the engineering audience. The transition stalls because the engineering fluency has not been demonstrated externally; the lead is read as a smart commissioner, not as an engineering manager.
- The engineering organisation runs on a technical-leadership culture the lead does not fit (deep IC coding expectations at manager altitude, specific language or framework specialisations, on-call rotation at a depth the lead has not built). This is organisation-specific and may not be addressable in the preparation window.
- The lead's Chapter 02 factor 1 read never actually pointed to this path; the signals are external (an engineering manager friend making an offer, the commissioning work being attractive) rather than internal (energy from the partnership, growth from the surface). External signals that do not match internal energy are usually not the right transition.

## Reading the signals across the quarterly review

The signals are not read on a calendar schedule; they are read against the quarterly review from [Chapter 03](03-the-deliberate-development-plan-toward-the-chosen-next-step.md). The quarterly review's four questions already include "what signalled in the chosen direction" and "what signalled against it." The signals in this chapter are the content of those questions.

Specifically: the lead re-reads this chapter's four sections against the quarter's evidence, each quarter. A pattern of three signals co-occurring over two consecutive quarters is the pattern that triggers the specific transition conversation with the sponsor. A pattern of mixed signals or a pattern of counter-signals is a pattern that extends the preparation window.

The chapter does not say "step in month 18" or "step in month 24." The honest read of the signals says when the step is at hand.

## The boundary this chapter respects

The chapter named the signals for the four paths; it does not name the signals for the moves it is not covering:

- **Staying at level 45 another three years.** A legitimate choice, with its own signals (the operating loop is still growing the lead, the organisation is still rewarding depth, the life-stage inputs make the stay honest). Not covered because the chapter's spec is the next-step transition.
- **Moving out of the four adjacent roles.** Some leads move into product leadership, consultancy partner tracks, academic positions, policy roles, or executive-education faculty. The signals for those moves are real but are outside the four paths the curriculum covers.
- **The interview process itself.** Signals tell the lead when to step; the interview process is the discrete skill that converts the signal into the step. The public artefact set ([Chapter 05](05-the-public-artifact-set-conference-talk-writing-open-source.md)) and the external credentials positioning ([Chapter 04](04-positioning-the-credentials-portfolio-externally.md)) feed it; the interview itself is beyond the curriculum's scope.

## Summary

- **The transition from level 45 to the next step happens at a moment identified by signals, not by a calendar date.** Three signals co-occurring over two to three quarters is the shape; one signal in one quarter is noise.
- **Each of the four paths has its own signal set, read against the current role's evidence:**
  - *To `head-of-ai-governance`:* the weekly surface is majority-governance, the governance counterpart treats the lead as a peer, the enablement work has a credible successor, the enablement scorecard is boring.
  - *To `chief-ai-officer`:* the CAIO seat is vacant and the sponsor is advocating, the board reads the lead's writing, the enablement function is one of several AI surfaces, the lead's own thesis is enterprise-level.
  - *To `senior-ai-governance-architect`:* the lead is being asked to produce the taxonomy, the engineering-peer surfaces are architectural, the lead reads standards for structure.
  - *To `ai-engineering-manager`:* the engineering peers are the primary partner surface, the lead is reviewing engineering decisions, the engineering manager has signalled sponsorship, the engineering-fluency artefact has shipped.
- **Each path has counter-signals that extend the preparation window.** Counter-signals are specific — an operating fragility, a missing credential, a sponsor that has not emerged, a preparation gap. Read them honestly; do not step on partial signal.
- **The signals are read quarterly as part of [Chapter 03](03-the-deliberate-development-plan-toward-the-chosen-next-step.md)'s review.** A pattern over two consecutive quarters, not a single quarter's reading, is the trigger.
- **The chapter authored the transition points, not the decision to stay.** Staying is a legitimate choice; the signals above help the lead who is considering the step make the decision at the right time.
