# Choosing the Next Step on the Ladder

> By mod-111 the role has reached steady state at level 45. The CoE is operating; the quarterly ritual is holding; the scorecard is credible; the portfolio of credentials is active in the room. The next question — the one the lead has been deferring for about eighteen months because the launch work did not leave time to ask it — is whether to stay at this altitude or go up, and if up, in which direction. This chapter is the decision framework for that question. It is not a career-coaching chapter; it is an authored read of the four adjacent next steps and the signatures that distinguish them, so the choice is deliberate and not opportunistic.

## Why the decision has to be made explicitly

A lead who does not choose their next step does not stand still. They drift. Enablement-lead work is cross-functional enough that opportunity will arrive from multiple directions — the governance side will want a program lead because they have liked the lead's redline discipline, the CAIO will want a deputy because the lead's story lands at board altitude, the engineering side will want a manager because the lead has commissioned well against their peers, the standards side will want an architect because the lead has internalised ISO/IEC 42001 clauses. Each ask will arrive in the form of a coffee, not a posting. Each will be real. Each will be a different career.

A lead who has not chosen in advance will say yes to the ask that arrives first, with the loudest sponsor, or in the quarter when the current role is most stale. That is not a bad decision — many good careers are made that way. But it is a decision made under pressure, and it under-invests in the specific preparation that each of the four paths rewards. The deliberate-development chapter ([Chapter 03](03-the-deliberate-development-plan-toward-the-chosen-next-step.md)) is where the preparation lives; this chapter is the choice that sets the preparation's direction.

The decision is not permanent. Many of the moves described below are reversible on a three-to-five-year horizon, and some careers thread through several of them. But the preparation window — the two to three years before the next-step hiring conversation lands — is where the decision is most expensive to defer. A lead eighteen months into a plan oriented toward `head-of-ai-governance` has very different artefacts and conversations in flight than a lead eighteen months into a plan oriented toward `ai-engineering-manager`, even if both are still in the level-45 role.

## The four adjacent next steps named

The CURRICULUM.md and [mod-101 Chapter 04](../mod-101-role-and-the-org-wide-enablement-operating-model/04-handoffs-with-adjacent-roles.md) name four adjacent roles the lead can realistically step into on a two-to-three-year horizon. Two are on the AI Governance ladder. One is on the AI Engineering ladder. The fourth is the executive position above the three.

### Path A — `head-of-ai-governance` (level 60, AI Governance family)

**What the role owns.** The AI governance program at organisation scope — policy taxonomy end-to-end, the control library the enablement program plugs into, regulator engagement, board reporting from the governance side, program-level incident ownership, cross-BU conformity oversight. A program-leadership position, not an architecture position.

**Shape of step-up.** Upward by one level (45 → 60) and across to the Governance family. A significant level jump; the enablement lead is one of several candidate pools (others are senior privacy officers, deputy GCs with AI exposure, standards professionals with program experience).

**What the enablement lead's track prepares.** The policy co-authoring ([mod-103](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/README.md)), the incident-path cross-work ([mod-109](../mod-109-ai-incident-response-and-enablement-side-rollback/README.md)), the quarterly council attendance ([mod-111 Chapter 05](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/05-the-quarterly-cross-functional-operating-ritual.md)), and the standards vocabulary (AIGP, ISO/IEC 42001) each build governance-adjacent surface. The cross-functional program-management muscle from mod-111 is directly transferable to running a governance program.

**Gaps the lead has to close.** Control-library authorship and taxonomy at the level the governance architect works at; direct regulator engagement experience (as distinct from feeding evidence into others' engagement); experience owning risk acceptance decisions at exec altitude; the audit-bearing posture that comes with certification conformity if the organisation certifies to ISO/IEC 42001.

**Signature evidence the next-step audience reads.** The redline log depth; the governance-council attendance record and contribution pattern; the incident post-mortems the lead co-authored; a Lead Implementer credential if the organisation is in certification scope.

### Path B — `senior-ai-governance-architect` (level 50, AI Governance family)

**What the role owns.** The control-library architecture and policy taxonomy — the controls that policy stacks plug into, the pattern language the architecture consumes, the standards-mapping documents, the red-team-to-control-library translation, the architectural decision records on policy shape. An architecture position, not a program-leadership position.

**Shape of step-up.** Upward by five levels (45 → 50) and across to the Governance family. A lateral-with-step shape — same approximate altitude as the current role but with a different discipline posture (architecture rather than program leadership).

**What the enablement lead's track prepares.** The policy-authoring depth ([mod-103](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/README.md), especially Chapter 03's NIST / ISO reading); the commissioning posture ([mod-110](../mod-110-commissioning-internal-ai-tools-and-custom-agents/README.md), which teaches architectural brief-writing at the altitude architects consume); the industry-flavour chapter ([mod-108](../mod-108-industry-and-regulatory-flavours-of-enablement/README.md)) which puts the lead in multi-regulator conversations where control-library mapping matters.

**Gaps the lead has to close.** Depth in a specific standards body's language — ISO/IEC 42001 clauses at Lead Implementer / Lead Auditor depth, NIST AI RMF categories at operating depth; architectural writing discipline (ADRs, decision-record patterns, taxonomy authorship); hands-on red-team-to-control literacy, often cross-trained from `ai-risk-engineer` at peer level.

**Signature evidence the next-step audience reads.** The depth of the policy stack's structure (controls separable from policies; mappings to standards explicit); the ADRs on policy shape the lead has authored; the commissioning briefs read as architectural specifications; the Lead Implementer / Lead Auditor credential if held.

### Path C — `chief-ai-officer` (level 70, AI Governance family)

**What the role owns.** Enterprise AI strategy, portfolio P&L, board positioning, M&A diligence on AI, portfolio-wide transformation, regulator-facing relationships at the CEO's altitude. An executive position — the role the enablement lead has been writing exec communication *to* for the last two years.

**Shape of step-up.** Upward by twenty-five levels (45 → 70) and across to the Governance family. A substantial step; the enablement lead is one of several candidate pools, usually alongside a sitting CIO, a sitting CFO with AI exposure, a sitting Chief Data Officer, or a Head of AI Governance already at level 60. Direct level-45-to-70 moves are rare but not unprecedented; more common is the level-45 → level-60 → level-70 trajectory.

**What the enablement lead's track prepares.** The exec-communication craft ([mod-106 Chapter 03](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/03-writing-for-executives-board-updates-lt-briefings-strategic-memos.md), [Chapter 01](01-the-exec-communication-story-that-ties-the-track-together.md)); the budget / ROI / build-vs-buy discipline ([mod-107](../mod-107-budget-roi-and-build-vs-buy-at-org-scope/README.md)); the cross-functional quarterly ritual ([mod-111 Chapter 05](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/05-the-quarterly-cross-functional-operating-ritual.md)); the honest-measurement discipline and board-ready narrative ([mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md)).

**Gaps the lead has to close.** P&L ownership (the enablement program carries a budget but not a P&L; the CAIO owns business-line-adjacent revenue and cost); M&A diligence experience; board-level governance scaffolding beyond the enablement seat; a leadership record that spans engineering, product, and go-to-market, not only enablement-side.

**Signature evidence the next-step audience reads.** Board-update authorship (not drafts — authored and signed artefacts); a quantified portfolio-level ROI narrative with confidence bands that the CFO has signed off on; cross-functional reorganisations the lead has driven; executive-education exposure at the business-school altitude; sponsorship by a sitting CAIO or C-suite peer.

### Path D — `ai-engineering-manager` (level 50, AI Engineering family)

**What the role owns.** Manager-of-managers scope on the AI Engineering ladder — teams of applied, agentic, and platform engineers, roadmap ownership, hiring and performance management of engineers at the IC and manager level, engineering partnership with product and governance. An engineering-side people-management role.

**Shape of step-up.** Upward by five levels (45 → 50) and across to the Engineering family. A sideways-with-step shape. The lead who takes this path has usually commissioned more than most leads — their R16 surface ([mod-110](../mod-110-commissioning-internal-ai-tools-and-custom-agents/README.md)) is deep, and the applied- and agentic-engineer peers at level 30 have become the lead's primary partner surface rather than one of several.

**What the enablement lead's track prepares.** The commissioning posture and brief-writing depth ([mod-110](../mod-110-commissioning-internal-ai-tools-and-custom-agents/README.md)); the program-management vocabulary ([mod-111](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/README.md), PMP-adjacent); the change-management and champion-network discipline ([mod-105](../mod-105-champion-networks-and-change-management/README.md)) translated to engineering-team adoption of AI tooling and practice.

**Gaps the lead has to close.** Engineering-specific technical fluency beyond the commissioning brief altitude — the AI-engineering-manager is reviewing code and architecture decisions, not just briefs; engineering-team hiring and performance management experience; engineering-culture work (on-call, incident engineering, deployment pipeline posture, production ownership). A lead who has not shipped code in years is a lead for whom this path has a hard technical-fluency floor.

**Signature evidence the next-step audience reads.** The commissioning brief depth (specifically, the no-go calls that cited engineering-side mechanisms rather than enablement-side); the quality of partnerships with the applied-ai-engineer peers; any engineering adjacency the lead carries (prior IC or team-lead engineering experience, open-source engineering artefacts, hands-on commissioning that went deeper than brief-to-acceptance).

## The decision framework — four factors, sequenced

The decision is not made by comparing the four paths side by side. That comparison is paralysing — each role is attractive from the altitude of the one next to it, and the lead's existing network will pull in at least two of the four directions simultaneously. The useful framework is sequenced, in four factors:

### Factor 1 — Which surface in the current role has the lead become most skilled at *and* most energised by

The current role's eleven surfaces are not uniformly fun to run. Every lead has two or three they look forward to on Monday morning and two or three they avoid. The next step rewards doubling down on the first set; it punishes stepping away from the second set into a role that is made of them.

Rough translations:

- **Policy, governance council, incident path, standards-reading surfaces energised** → path A (`head-of-ai-governance`) or path B (`senior-ai-governance-architect`).
- **Exec writing, budget defense, board narrative, cross-functional reorganisation energised** → path C (`chief-ai-officer`).
- **Commissioning briefs, applied-/agentic-engineer partnerships, tool-architecture conversations energised** → path D (`ai-engineering-manager`).
- **Training delivery, champion coaching, fluency assessment energised** → likely a stay-in-role-and-deepen pattern, or an L&D leadership path outside the four here; worth naming to the lead that there is no obvious adjacent level-50-plus role made of these surfaces.

The honest read on this factor is harder than it sounds. Leads will often identify "what I am best at" rather than "what I am most energised by," and the two are not the same. The energy question is what sustains the five-year commitment the next step requires; the skill question is what gets the interview.

### Factor 2 — Where the lead's most load-bearing partnerships sit at steady state

The quarterly ritual from [mod-111 Chapter 05](../mod-111-ai-coe-operations-vendor-management-and-cross-functional-leadership/05-the-quarterly-cross-functional-operating-ritual.md) puts the lead in standing partnerships with multiple surfaces. By year two, one or two of those partnerships will have become notably deeper than the rest — the governance counterpart the lead has co-authored eight policies with, the applied-ai-engineer peer the lead has commissioned four tools with, the CAIO who has taken the lead's advice on three sponsor-level decisions, the CPO who has let the lead's playbook library into the formal L&D curriculum.

The deepest partnership is often a signal of the natural next step. Not because the partner will hire the lead (though they might), but because the partnership shape is the shape the next role trades in daily.

### Factor 3 — The organisation's own next-step shape

The four next-step roles are not always available inside the current organisation. In a 4,000-person consultancy, `head-of-ai-governance` may not exist at level 60 — the GC's deputy carries the surface at lower altitude, and the step up requires leaving. In an 8,000-person health system, `chief-ai-officer` may exist in title only, with the actual AI strategy owned by the CIO — the step up is inside the CIO's org, not into a CAIO seat. In a 12,000-person bank, all four roles typically exist separately and at full altitude.

The lead reads the organisation's current shape and names, honestly, which paths are available inside the organisation and which require an external move. An external move is not a failure; it is a different horizon. The two-to-three-year preparation looks different for an external search than for an internal step — the external search weights the public artifact set ([Chapter 05](05-the-public-artifact-set-conference-talk-writing-open-source.md)) heavily, the internal step weights the sponsor-network work more.

### Factor 4 — The compensation and life-stage reality

The four paths have different compensation trajectories, different travel loads, different board-exposure loads, different work-rhythm shapes. In general the CAIO path (level 70) sits at the top of the band and carries the highest board-exposure load; the senior architect path (level 50) is often the most intellectually satisfying but has a slower exec-pay progression; the governance-program-lead and engineering-manager paths sit in the middle on most dimensions. Specific band figures vary across geography, industry, and organisation size — the lead triangulates against the most recent state pay-transparency postings (California, Colorado, New York, Washington, Illinois) rather than a single published number.

The life-stage reality — small children at home, a partner's career constraints, a geographic tie, a health posture — is a real input to the decision. A lead who is honest about the life-stage inputs picks the path that compounds rather than depletes. A lead who ignores the inputs picks the path that looks best in the quarterly-review language and discovers in year three that the role does not fit the life.

## The decision instrument

The chapter's exercise ([exercise-02](exercises/exercise-02-next-step-ladder-decision-worked-example.md)) runs the decision as a worked example. The instrument the exercise produces is a one-page decision memo with five sections:

1. **The honest read on factor 1** — two or three sentences on which surfaces energise the lead and which drain them, with specific evidence from the last four quarters.
2. **The partnership map on factor 2** — the three deepest standing partnerships, named with the partner role and the artefacts co-produced.
3. **The organisational shape on factor 3** — whether the four paths exist inside the organisation and at what altitude; what an external move would look like.
4. **The life-stage read on factor 4** — honest, personal, not for circulation outside the lead's own notes.
5. **The decision itself** — one path named, two sentences on why, and the horizon (2 years, 3 years, "open to signal in year 1").

The decision memo is a *personal artefact*, not a work artefact. It does not ship to the sponsor; it does not go in the standing cadence. It sits in the lead's own notes and is re-read once a quarter, usually in the week after the quarterly ritual closes, and updated if any of the four factors has shifted.

## When the decision shifts

The chosen path is not fixed for the full horizon. Specific signals legitimately shift the decision:

- **A new partner surface deepens unexpectedly.** The sponsor rotation brings in a CAIO who takes the lead under their wing in a way the previous sponsor did not. A lead who had been orienting toward path B (architect) re-reads toward path C (CAIO deputy) because the sponsor-sponsorship surface is now load-bearing.
- **A major incident changes the lead's surface load.** An AI incident pulls the lead deep into the governance-side work for two quarters. A lead who had been orienting toward path D (engineering manager) re-reads the incident experience and recognises that the governance work has been the energising surface after all.
- **The organisation's shape changes.** The organisation makes a major acquisition, and the combined entity has a different executive layer. A lead who had been orienting toward an internal level-60 step reads the acquisition and re-orients toward an external search.
- **A life-stage input shifts.** A move, a family change, a health change. The pre-existing read on factor 4 is no longer valid; the decision has to be re-run.

The re-read is quarterly. The decision does not change on every quarterly re-read — the preparation window is long enough that flipping between paths twice a year is its own failure mode. But a quarterly honest look at whether the current path still fits is the discipline that keeps the preparation aligned.

## What this chapter does not decide

Three things this chapter deliberately does not decide:

- **Whether to stay in role.** Staying at level 45 in a mature enablement program is a legitimate choice. The role can deepen for several years without stepping up; the stay-and-deepen pattern is not covered here because the chapter's spec is "the next step," but a lead whose factor 1, 2, 3, 4 honest read is "actually I would stay here another three years" has made a valid choice and does not need the next chapters' preparation plan.
- **Whether to leave for a non-adjacent role.** Some leads move out of enablement into product leadership, consultancy partner tracks, academia, or policy work. These are not covered here — the chapter's four paths are the *adjacent* next steps; the non-adjacent moves live outside the curriculum.
- **The hiring conversation itself.** The chapter is the orientation; the hiring conversation is a discrete skill that the public artefact set ([Chapter 05](05-the-public-artifact-set-conference-talk-writing-open-source.md)) and the external-credentials positioning ([Chapter 04](04-positioning-the-credentials-portfolio-externally.md)) feed. The decision happens before the conversation; the conversation is a consequence, not the trigger.

## Summary

- **The next step on the ladder is a decision the lead has to make deliberately, not drift into.** A drifted-into step under-invests the two-to-three-year preparation window the next-step hiring conversation rewards.
- **Four adjacent next steps are available at the level-45 lead's horizon:** `head-of-ai-governance` (60, program leadership), `senior-ai-governance-architect` (50, control-library architecture), `chief-ai-officer` (70, executive scope), `ai-engineering-manager` (50, engineering people management). Each is reached from a different orientation of the eleven current-role surfaces.
- **Four factors drive the decision, sequenced:** which surfaces energise the lead, where the deepest standing partnerships sit, which paths are available inside the current organisation, and the honest life-stage reality.
- **The decision instrument is a personal one-page memo** re-read quarterly and updated when any of the four factors shifts. It does not ship externally; it anchors the preparation plan in [Chapter 03](03-the-deliberate-development-plan-toward-the-chosen-next-step.md).
- **The decision is not permanent, but the preparation window is where it is most expensive to defer.** Re-run the memo quarterly; let legitimate signals shift it; do not flip between paths twice a year.
