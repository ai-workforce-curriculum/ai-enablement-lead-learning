# The Rollout Communications Cadence

> A rollout communications cadence that keeps the executive scorecard honest answers four questions on a recurring schedule — *what is being built, what is available, what is working, what should employees do next* — against the same artifacts the scorecard reads from. Miss any one of the four and the scorecard drifts into marketing; hit all four and the cadence is the primary defense of the program's credibility.

## The four slots, in order

R9 in the role evidence (0.69, 18 of 26 postings) names the communications cadence as an explicit enablement-lead accountability — present at nearly 70% of mature role descriptions. Across those postings, the cadence collapses into four durable content slots:

1. **What is being built** — the forward-looking slot. Pilots in flight, tool evaluations underway, policy changes in redline, training content in production.
2. **What is available** — the current-state slot. The tools in the approved envelope today, the policies in force today, the training pathways available today.
3. **What is working** — the outcome slot. Named use cases, named functions, named champions. The field-report attribution surface from [Chapter 03](03-routing-field-signal-back-to-policy-tooling-and-training.md) feeds this slot directly.
4. **What employees should do next** — the call-to-action slot. The specific operator-visible next step: register for the cohort, join the pilot, read the updated SOP, attend the office hour.

The four slots run in order because that is the order an employee reads them in. "Something new is coming (1), here is what we have today (2), here is what others are getting from it (3), here is your next action (4)." The order matters — flip (2) and (3) and the content reads like marketing; drop (4) and the whole thing is a newsletter.

## Why each slot earns its place

Each slot carries a specific load. Dropping any one produces a predictable failure.

### Slot 1 — What is being built

This slot keeps the organization oriented on the trajectory, not just the present. Without it, employees experience every rollout as a surprise — and surprise is a resistance driver ([Chapter 06](06-ai-specific-resistance-patterns-adkar-and-kotter.md)).

Content:

- Tools in Stage 1 / Stage 2 of the three-stage selection ([mod-102 Chapter 01](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/01-the-three-stage-selection-decision.md)) that are likely to graduate this quarter or next.
- Policy changes under walking-redline ([mod-103 Chapter 01](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/01-the-co-author-posture-and-the-redline-log.md)) — with enough shape that functions can prepare, not so much detail that pre-decisional drafts leak.
- Training content in production (new playbooks, new spotlights, upcoming bootcamp cohorts).
- Internal tool commissioning in progress ([mod-110](../mod-110-commissioning-internal-ai-tools-and-custom-agents/README.md)), at a level of detail appropriate to each audience.

**What not to put here:** speculative items the sponsor has not committed to. The slot is "being built," not "being considered." Credibility of the whole cadence depends on things that appear here actually shipping; a 70% ship rate is the floor.

### Slot 2 — What is available

The current-state slot is the one operators actually use. It answers the single most-asked question in the organization: "what am I allowed to use, right now, to do my job?"

Content:

- The current approved-tool envelope with named entry points (login URL, provisioning route, support path).
- The current acceptable-use policy and data-handling SOP, with a dated changelog.
- The training pathways available today, by role, with the specific next step for each.
- The current champion roster, by function.

The slot is maintained in the self-serve layer ([mod-104 Chapter 05](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/05-the-delivery-mix.md)) and *referenced* in the cadence, not duplicated in it. Each cadence send points to the live self-serve surface — one source of truth, one date stamp.

**What not to put here:** things that are in pilot, in review, or with named caveats that break on day two. If it needs an asterisk, it goes in Slot 1 (being built) until it can appear here cleanly.

### Slot 3 — What is working

The outcome slot defends the program against the CFO question and feeds the executive scorecard narrative ([mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md)). It is also the slot operators read most skeptically; it earns credibility or burns it.

Content:

- Specific use cases, with the function, the operator (named with consent), the before-and-after shape, and the measurement.
- Field-report attribution — "the acceptable-use policy clarification on section 4 came from reports by named champions in supply chain and legal ops" (from [Chapter 03](03-routing-field-signal-back-to-policy-tooling-and-training.md)).
- Scorecard movement — adoption curve milestones, incident closure, training completion.
- Dissent and dead-ends. **This is the discipline that keeps the slot honest.** Programs that only report success get discounted by the organization quickly. A paragraph each quarter about the pilot that was killed, the tool that didn't make the envelope, the policy that didn't work and was reverted — this is what the audience treats as signal that the rest is honest.

**What not to put here:** vendor-provided ROI numbers, aggregate industry benchmarks, counter-factual projections. The slot is "what is working *here, in this organization.*" If the only evidence is external, there is no evidence yet.

### Slot 4 — What employees should do next

Every cadence send closes with action. Not a fade-out, not a "let us know if you have questions" — an action.

Content, per audience segment:

- Operators: the one next thing (register for the cohort, join the pilot, update your workflow to the new SOP, attend the office hour).
- Specialists: the signal they should watch for in their function.
- Champions: the next field-report cycle, the next network call, the next bootcamp.
- Leadership: the scorecard review, the governance council agenda, the budget review.

One next step per audience. Multiple next steps per audience is a sign the content was written by section without thinking about the reader; a reader with five "next steps" will do none.

## The cadence surfaces — who reads what, how often

The four slots run across multiple surfaces at different cadences. They are not one email. Treat them as a content system that produces different artifacts for different audiences.

| Surface | Audience | Frequency | What it carries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly org-wide update | All employees in scope | Monthly | Light touch on all four slots; operator-focused Slot 4 |
| Quarterly function brief | Function leadership + their teams | Quarterly | Function-specific cut of all four slots; function-specific Slot 4 |
| Quarterly executive scorecard | Exec sponsor, governance council | Quarterly | Slot 3 heavy, with Slot 1 trajectory and Slot 4 as decisions requested |
| Standing champion-network call | Champion roster | Monthly | All four slots with field-side emphasis |
| Self-serve layer | Everyone, continuous | Continuous | Slot 2 lives here canonically |
| Launch comms (per rollout) | Function-of-the-rollout | Event-driven | Deep Slot 1→2 transition, Slot 4 heavy |
| Policy change notice | All employees | Event-driven | Slot 2 update with Slot 4 ("what you do differently starting Monday") |

Note what is *not* on this list: ad-hoc all-hands slides, one-off email blasts, surprise executive announcements. The cadence is a cadence because it is recurring; irregular communications degrade the recurring ones by competing for the same attention budget.

## The exec scorecard slot — the one that keeps the cadence honest

The quarterly exec scorecard is the surface the exec sponsor reads. [mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md) treats the full scorecard design; the slice this chapter covers is the content handoff from the communications cadence into it.

The scorecard is the slot where Slot 3 (what is working) is most load-bearing. Three disciplines keep it honest:

- **Named functions, named operators (with consent), named outcomes.** Not "productivity up 15%." Not "operators report positive sentiment." A specific function, a specific workflow, a measurement on the actual before-and-after.
- **The failed-attempt paragraph.** One paragraph per scorecard cycle naming a pilot that was killed, a tool that didn't make the envelope, a policy that didn't work. Boards and exec sponsors do not read this as weakness; they read it as evidence that the rest is honest. The alternative — all wins, every quarter — has a short half-life.
- **The ask.** Every scorecard has an ask: a decision the exec sponsor needs to make, a budget line that needs confirmation, a cross-functional dependency that needs unblocking. A scorecard with no ask has stopped being a decision document.

The handoff from the cadence into the scorecard is not a copy-paste. The cadence carries the organization's conversation; the scorecard carries the exec sponsor's. The content overlaps; the shape does not.

## Partnership with internal comms

The enablement lead does not own the organization's communication channels — internal comms does. The cadence runs through them, not around them. [mod-104 Chapter 08](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/08-partnering-with-hr-ld-and-internal-comms.md) treats the partnership contract in detail; the slice here is the specific communications cadence handoff.

Three things the partnership contract does for the cadence:

- **Calendar discipline.** The monthly / quarterly rhythm fits into the organization's broader communications calendar; the enablement cadence does not blast past earnings-release weeks, acquisition-announcement days, or executive town-hall moments.
- **Channel competence.** Internal comms knows which channels the audience actually reads. The enablement lead knows what the content should be. Neither authors the other's piece.
- **Standards.** Accessibility, translation, inclusive language. The enablement lead is not the subject-matter expert here; internal comms owns the standards and the cadence fits them.

The anti-pattern is the enablement lead writing for channels internal comms does not support, or internal comms re-writing the content into vendor marketing prose. The partnership contract is where both sides prevent both patterns.

## Three cadence failure shapes

Each has a specific shape and cause.

- **The marketing-newsletter cadence.** All four slots present, but Slot 3 is unsourced claims ("productivity gains across the organization") and Slot 4 is soft. The audience reads it like marketing and skims. Cause: the field-signal engine from [Chapter 03](03-routing-field-signal-back-to-policy-tooling-and-training.md) is not feeding Slot 3, so the slot is filled with projection instead of evidence.
- **The pilot-announcement cadence.** Slot 1 is overweight — every month there is another new tool "being built" — and Slot 2 (available) doesn't move. The organization experiences the program as perpetually about-to-arrive. Cause: the pilot-to-GA pipeline from [mod-102](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/README.md) is stuck, and the cadence is masking the stall with new announcements.
- **The silent cadence.** The cadence lapses — a quarter skipped, then another. The organization fills the vacuum with vendor marketing from their own inboxes. Cause: the operations analyst was overloaded, the ops rhythm was not protected, or the exec sponsor stopped reviewing the scorecard and the pull disappeared.

Each failure is visible in the health dashboard from [Chapter 07](07-measuring-change-management-health.md); each has a specific intervention there.

## The dissent slot — the smallest, most load-bearing element

Inside the cadence, one small element does disproportionate work: the explicit surface for dissent. A paragraph in Slot 3 (what is working) that names a pilot that was killed. A line in the champion-network call that gives the floor to a concern. A section in the exec scorecard for "unresolved issues the program faces."

This is the single element that makes the cadence credible. An organization reading a communications cadence can tell within two issues whether the surface is honest. The dissent slot is how it tells.

Without the dissent slot, the cadence is read as marketing. With it, the cadence is read as reporting. The difference in the organization's response is large.

## Summary

- Four content slots, in order — *being built, available, working, next.* R9 (0.69, 18/26 postings) names the cadence; the four-slot structure is the durable shape across the evidence.
- Each slot carries a specific load; dropping any one produces a predictable failure (perpetual-coming, newsletter-of-the-present, unsourced-claims, no-action).
- The cadence runs across multiple surfaces at different frequencies — not one email, a content system — through the internal comms partnership, not around it.
- The exec scorecard is the surface that most needs Slot 3 discipline: named functions, named operators, measurement on actual before-and-after, a failed-attempt paragraph, and an ask.
- The dissent slot is small and load-bearing; it is the element that differentiates reporting from marketing.
- Three cadence failures — marketing-newsletter, pilot-announcement, silent — each have a specific cause reachable through the health dashboard in [Chapter 07](07-measuring-change-management-health.md).
