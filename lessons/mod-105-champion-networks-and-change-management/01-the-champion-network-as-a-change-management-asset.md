# The Champion Network as a Change-Management Asset

> A champion network is not a training ambassador program, not a product evangelist cohort, and not an internal user group for the vendor. It is a *change-management asset* — a named, trained, manager-released set of humans who make policy and tooling real in local context and who carry field signal back up where policy and tooling get changed. If the network only runs in one direction, it is a marketing channel. If it runs in both, it is the single most load-bearing asset the enablement lead operates.

## The frame the rest of the module rests on

Two framings of the same artifact; different programs follow.

- **Champions as broadcasters.** The CoE writes the content, the champions carry it to their function, the measurement is attendance and reach. The signal direction is one-way — out of the centre, into the field. This is the shape most "AI champion program" job descriptions imply, and it is the shape that produces a network that burns out in the second year.
- **Champions as a two-way field interface.** Champions carry the centre's content outward *and* the field's signal inward. The measurement is both reach *and* the volume of policy / tooling / training changes that originated in a champion report. This is the shape that compounds — because the artifacts the champions carry get better every quarter, because the field trusts the artifacts more every quarter, and because the champions see their own fingerprints on the program and stay.

Pick the first framing and the job description writes itself around *training* verbs — brief, present, demo, evangelise. Pick the second and the verbs go in both directions — *brief* and *surface*, *present* and *escalate*, *demo* and *report*. R8 in the role evidence (0.54, 14/26 postings surveyed) names the second framing; this module is written against the second.

## What a champion IS, in one sentence

A champion is **a named operator inside a specific function, trained ahead of general availability, whose manager has agreed in writing to the time allocation, who is the local first-line answer for that function's AI questions and whose field reports feed the centre's policy, tooling, and training backlog.**

Unpack the five load-bearing words:

- **Named.** A list of specific humans, with a name against each function or business unit. Not "the sales team's champion" — "Priya Shah, sales operations, Americas." Anonymity defeats the point.
- **Trained ahead of GA.** A champion walked onto day one of the rollout already fluent in the tool, the policy, the escalation path, and the use cases that the centre has approved for their function. If training happens *after* GA, the champion is a user like everyone else and the role collapses.
- **Manager-released.** The champion's manager has signed off on the time commitment — typically 10–20% of a working week — and has accepted that during the rollout window this is how the champion's performance is partly measured. Without this, champions are *volunteers* who quietly deprioritise the work.
- **Local first-line answer.** When an operator in that function has a question, the champion is who they ask *first*. Not the CoE Slack channel, not the vendor's documentation, not the all-hands recording. The champion.
- **Field report into the backlog.** Everything the champion sees — the use cases that worked, the ones that didn't, the policy questions the centre's AUP did not answer, the operators who tried a tool off-envelope — flows back into a specific intake that the CoE operates. Chapter 03 treats the mechanics.

If any of the five is missing, you have a champion program *in name*. The work you get out of it is a function of the ones you actually have.

## What a champion is NOT

Three roles get confused with the champion role and each confusion is worth naming.

- **Not a trainer.** Delivery of the foundational curriculum and role-based playbooks belongs to L&D and to `ai-augmented-team-manager` L40 (see [mod-104 Chapter 08](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/08-partnering-with-hr-ld-and-internal-comms.md)). A champion may *co-facilitate* a workshop for their function, but running the curriculum is not their job. If the champion role drifts into "the function's AI trainer," the champion is doing two jobs and will leave.
- **Not an evangelist.** The champion's job is not to generate enthusiasm; it is to make the tooling and policy work for the function and to feed back what isn't working. A champion who reports only success is not functioning as a two-way interface — they are functioning as marketing, and the centre loses the field signal it needs to improve the program.
- **Not a vendor advocate.** Vendors love champion networks because they look like unpaid product advocacy. The champion works for the organization, not the tool. When the tool stops serving the function, the champion's job is to say so — ideally to the centre, in writing, in the next field report.

The last confusion is the most expensive. A champion network that has become a vendor advocacy group has lost its independence; the first time the tool needs to be deprioritised or replaced, the champions become an obstacle to the decision instead of the people who surface the need for it.

## Why the two-way signal matters at the operating-model level

The enablement operating model from [mod-101 Chapter 03](../mod-101-role-and-the-org-wide-enablement-operating-model/03-authoring-the-enablement-operating-model.md) has three failure modes if its inputs dry up. The champion network is the primary mitigation for all three.

- **Policy that drifts from practice.** The acceptable-use policy and data-handling SOP ([mod-103 Chapter 04](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/04-the-one-page-acceptable-use-policy.md) and [Chapter 05](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/05-the-data-handling-sop.md)) are written at the centre. They encounter reality in the field. Without a field signal, the gap between written policy and operator practice widens quarter by quarter; the first evidence the centre receives is an incident.
- **Tooling choices made against the wrong workload.** The three-stage selection decision from [mod-102 Chapter 01](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/01-the-three-stage-selection-decision.md) runs on signal about what operators are actually trying to do. If that signal comes only from vendor-briefed executives, the envelope drifts toward enterprise-friendly tools that operators do not use. Champions correct this — they name the workloads the envelope does not cover.
- **Training content that misses the function.** Role-based playbooks ([mod-104 Chapter 04](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/04-role-based-playbooks-and-use-case-libraries.md)) are only as good as the examples they contain. The examples come from champions. Without the champion loop, the playbooks stay generic and the function stops referencing them.

Chapter 03 treats the field-signal routing in operational detail. The frame to hold now: three adjacent systems depend on champion signal to stay honest, and if the signal stops, each of them degrades on its own timetable.

## The R8 evidence and what it says and doesn't say

R8 appears in 14 of the 26 enablement-lead job postings sampled in the role evidence (0.54 frequency). That tells us:

- A clear majority of mature enablement roles explicitly contract for a champion network. If you join one of these roles, running the network is one of your first-year accountabilities.
- A material minority (12 of 26) does not name the network explicitly in the job description. In most of those organizations, the network still exists — it goes by a different name ("power user program," "AI ambassadors," "digital champions") — and the shape is similar.
- The frequency is not strong enough to call champion networks universal. In very small organizations (under 500 people, roughly), the network often collapses into a single CoE member plus three or four heavy users. Formalising the network at that scale is overhead.

What R8 does not tell us: how to run the network well. That is what Chapters 02–07 treat. R8 justifies the chapter's existence; the content is grounded in change-management literature (Prosci ADKAR and Kotter 8-Step, treated in Chapter 06) and in the artifacts the role already operates (the launch-gate champion list from [mod-102 Chapter 03](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/03-the-rollout-artifact-set.md)).

## Where the launch-gate champion list meets the network

[mod-102 Chapter 03](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/03-the-rollout-artifact-set.md) names the champion list as one of seven rollout artifacts that must exist and be co-signed *before* GA. That artifact is the per-rollout slice of the network; this module treats the *standing* network that persists across rollouts.

The two are related but not identical:

- The **launch-gate champion list** is per-tool. It answers "who is the named, trained, manager-released first-line answer for tool X, function Y, on day one of GA?" It has an expiry — it is a launch-readiness artifact.
- The **standing champion network** is per-function (and sometimes per-business-unit). It persists across tools and policy versions. Its roster changes over time as members rotate in and out; its *shape* is stable.

The relationship: each new rollout draws its launch-gate list from the standing network. If the standing network is healthy, the launch-gate list is a 10-minute exercise. If it is not, every rollout turns into a champion recruitment project, and the rollout timeline absorbs that latency.

## Three recognisable network failure shapes

Each has a specific cause; each has a recognisable field smell. Chapters 02 and 07 give the operating mechanics; name them now so the rest of the module has shared vocabulary.

- **The social-club network.** High attendance at the monthly community-of-practice call; champions enjoy the company of other champions; no reports flow into the backlog. The network has become an end in itself. Cause: no accountability on the field-report side of the lifecycle. Smell: champions can name other champions by first name but cannot name the last policy change their reports contributed to.
- **The evangelist network.** Champions carry the centre's content outward well — launch comms, playbooks, spotlights — but the signal going inward is sanitised. Field reports are universally positive; incidents surface from elsewhere. Cause: the champion role is being evaluated on adoption *up* rather than signal *in*; champions optimise accordingly. Smell: the centre finds out about an issue from a team manager or an incident, not from a champion, even though a champion was present.
- **The crickets network.** The roster exists on paper; the champions are named; nothing happens. Office hours are empty, reports are not filed, the Slack channel has three messages this month. Cause: managers did not release the time, or the training was thin, or the recognition was absent, or all three. Smell: a champion listed against function X cannot remember when they last did a champion-specific action.

Each failure has a specific intervention. Chapter 07's measurement stack is designed to catch all three before they become structural.

## Summary

- A champion network is a *change-management asset*, not a training ambassador program; the load-bearing feature is two-way signal flow, not one-way broadcast.
- A champion is **named, trained ahead of GA, manager-released, the local first-line answer, and a reporter of field signal into the centre's backlog.** All five words earn their place; drop any one and the role collapses.
- Three confusions to avoid: trainer, evangelist, vendor advocate. The last is the most expensive.
- Three adjacent systems depend on champion signal to stay honest: policy (mod-103), tooling (mod-102), and training (mod-104). The network is the primary mitigation when these drift from practice.
- R8 (0.54, 14/26 postings) justifies the chapter; the content is grounded in change-management literature and in the launch-gate champion list from [mod-102 Chapter 03](../mod-102-selecting-and-rolling-out-ai-tools/03-the-rollout-artifact-set.md).
- Three failure shapes — social-club, evangelist, crickets — each have a specific cause and a specific intervention that the rest of the module builds out.
