# Quiz 01 — Champion Networks, Cadence, Opt-In vs. Mandate, Resistance, Health, and the Incident Handoff

**Format:** multiple choice · **Questions:** 12 · **Estimated effort:** 25–30 minutes
**Grounding:** [Chapter 01](../01-the-champion-network-as-a-change-management-asset.md), [Chapter 02](../02-the-four-phase-lifecycle-recruit-train-recognise-retire.md), [Chapter 03](../03-routing-field-signal-back-to-policy-tooling-and-training.md), [Chapter 04](../04-the-rollout-communications-cadence.md), [Chapter 05](../05-opt-in-vs-mandate.md), [Chapter 06](../06-ai-specific-resistance-patterns-adkar-and-kotter.md), [Chapter 07](../07-measuring-change-management-health.md), [Chapter 08](../08-the-handoff-to-the-governance-incident-path.md)

## How to take this quiz

Pick the single best answer for each question. Each question tests whether you can *apply* a chapter idea — paraphrasing the chapter is not enough. Answer key with short explanations is at the bottom; resist the urge to read it until you have your own answers written down.

### Q1. A new AI Enablement Lead inherits a "champion program" where 18 champions are listed on a SharePoint page, their training was a one-hour deck shown the week after each tool went GA, no manager-release paperwork exists, and the primary measure is "how many champions attended the quarterly networking dinner." Which of Chapter 01's five load-bearing words of the champion definition is *least* in evidence?

- **A.** Named.
- **B.** Trained ahead of GA.
- **C.** Manager-released.
- **D.** Field report into the backlog.

### Q2. A function's champion carries launch comms faithfully, posts spotlight stories in Slack monthly, and reports "adoption is going great" every quarter. Then a governance incident surfaces from a team manager's escalation — the champion was present in the function the whole time but had not flagged the issue. Under Chapter 01's three failure shapes, this is most clearly:

- **A.** The social-club network — champions enjoy each other's company and do not do work.
- **B.** The evangelist network — the outbound signal is strong, the inbound signal has been sanitised.
- **C.** The crickets network — the roster exists on paper but nothing operates.
- **D.** None of the three; a missed-escalation is a Chapter 08 handoff failure, not a network failure.

### Q3. Chapter 02 names four sourcing channels for champion recruitment in priority order. The chapter is explicit that **one channel sits first** because its signal reflects observed behaviour rather than self-selection, filter-gaming, or completion-of-compliance. That channel is:

- **A.** Open self-nomination (with a manager co-signature at submission).
- **B.** HR / L&D data — foundational-curriculum completion plus office-hours attendance.
- **C.** Team-manager nomination.
- **D.** Field signal from existing champions in adjacent functions.

### Q4. Chapter 02 names three recognition channels — visibility into the organization, visibility into the champion's own career, and visibility into the program's direction. The chapter says one of the three is the channel most programs under-invest in and is the one that actually retains champions **beyond a first term**. That channel is:

- **A.** Visibility into the organization — the champion's name appears in launch comms and the exec scorecard.
- **B.** Visibility into the champion's own career — the role is a named accountability on the performance review.
- **C.** Visibility into the program's direction — the champion sees their own fingerprints on shipped policy, tooling, and training changes.
- **D.** Material recognition — bonuses, swag, and the annual cohort dinner.

### Q5. A champion files a field report. The operations analyst replies "Thanks for the feedback — we've noted it" within 24 hours and adds the report to a running document that the CoE reviews "when time allows." No ticket is opened in any backlog, no route is named, no resolution date is given. Chapter 03 is explicit that this specific shape is:

- **A.** Acceptable for early-career champions still building reporting skills; routing comes later.
- **B.** The anti-pattern that breaks the two-way loop — reports become grievances to acknowledge rather than inputs to the backlog, and filings dry up inside a quarterly cycle.
- **C.** Correct — the report belongs in the shared drive until a cross-report pattern is confirmed.
- **D.** Acceptable only when the report lacks an operator-consent-to-attribute flag.

### Q6. A champion reports that operators in their function keep asking whether they can paste customer data into an approved writing assistant; the AUP is unclear on it; and the role-based playbook does not demo the policy-compliant workflow. Chapter 03's cross-routing treatment says the triage output should:

- **A.** Route to policy only — the tool is fine, the policy carries the clarification.
- **B.** Route to training only — the playbook is where the operator finds the workflow.
- **C.** Route to tooling — the tool's data-handling posture is the real issue.
- **D.** Route to both policy and training, coordinated to ship together — if the policy change ships without the training update, operators learn the new policy from the old material, which is worse than no policy change at all.

### Q7. Chapter 04 names four content slots for the rollout communications cadence — *what is being built, what is available, what is working, what employees should do next.* The chapter is explicit that the four slots run **in that order** because that is the order an employee reads them in. Which of the following swaps most predictably turns the cadence into marketing?

- **A.** Flipping *what is being built* (Slot 1) and *what is available* (Slot 2) — the audience reads the trajectory before the current state.
- **B.** Flipping *what is available* (Slot 2) and *what is working* (Slot 3) — the audience reads outcomes before current state, so the content reads like a vendor case-study deck.
- **C.** Flipping *what is working* (Slot 3) and *what employees should do next* (Slot 4) — the audience is asked to act before they see outcomes.
- **D.** Dropping Slot 1 entirely — the cadence becomes current-state only.

### Q8. The exec sponsor pushes back on a draft quarterly scorecard: "This is depressing — you've got a paragraph in here about a pilot you killed and another about a policy you reverted. Take it out; the board reads this and the room wants wins." Based on Chapter 04's exec-scorecard discipline, your most grounded response is:

- **A.** Remove the paragraph; the sponsor reads the room better than you do and the board deck is a sales surface.
- **B.** Keep the paragraph; the failed-attempt discipline is the single element that keeps Slot 3 honest, and an all-wins scorecard has a short half-life — boards and exec sponsors read the failed-attempt paragraph as evidence that the rest is honest.
- **C.** Replace the paragraph with a vendor benchmark showing the industry average failure rate — contextualise the losses with external data.
- **D.** Move the paragraph to an appendix only the governance council sees — keep the board-facing surface positive.

### Q9. An exec sponsor says: "Let's call it opt-in in the launch comms — no one wants a mandate on the first big AI rollout — but privately I'll tell team managers to lean on anyone who hasn't logged in by week six, and we'll quietly bake tool-use into the next quarterly KPI." Under Chapter 05, this is specifically:

- **A.** A defensible three-tier hybrid — Tier 1 Required for team managers, Tier 3 Available for everyone else.
- **B.** A reasonable opt-in with light enforcement; the only risk is communications messaging.
- **C.** The *soft-mandate* anti-pattern — announced as opt-in, enforced as mandate; the organization reads the shift quickly and trust in the enablement function erodes more than a plain mandate would have cost.
- **D.** A legitimate opt-in-to-mandate reversal if adoption lags — provided the reversal is named in the cadence.

### Q10. A senior engineer declines to attend the function's AI onboarding workshop: "I've been using ChatGPT at home for a year. I don't need a two-hour primer on how to prompt." Under Chapter 06's diagnostic walk, the chapter's primary intervention is:

- **A.** Make the workshop mandatory for all engineers regardless of prior experience; consistency matters more than individual fluency.
- **B.** Rework the training opening to name explicitly what the session *is* and *is not* — your consumer skill is a starting point; this three hours is about the organization's data-handling boundaries, approved-tool envelope, escalation paths, and function-specific use-case patterns — and route the engineer into the specialist-tier fast-track so they do not sit through foundational content a second time.
- **C.** Escalate the refusal to the engineer's manager as a compliance issue.
- **D.** Remove the workshop requirement for anyone who self-declares prior experience with consumer AI tools.

### Q11. The one-page health dashboard shows: active-use curve rising steadily (good S-curve shape), champion retention on baseline, communications reach on baseline, *and* the quarterly resistance survey's quality-skepticism signal rising in the free-text responses. Under Chapter 07's combined-signal reads, the most likely diagnosis is:

- **A.** All signals except one are healthy; ignore the survey outlier and protect the working rhythms.
- **B.** The tool is not finding workflow fit — route to a mod-102 selection re-examination.
- **C.** The silent-compliance risk is forming — operators are using the tool but not trusting it. Intervene before the trust gap closes toward "I'll use it badly and move on," pairing the curve with verification-habit reinforcement and a targeted investigation of the specific failure cases surfacing in the survey.
- **D.** A role-threat narrative is competing with the organizational vision — intervene in Slot 3 of the cadence.

### Q12. A champion files a field report tagged `incident-shaped`: an operator in their function pasted a customer's full name and address into a personal ChatGPT account because the approved tool's turnaround felt too slow, and two other operators appear to be doing the same thing. Under Chapter 08's four-step handoff, which of the following is something the *enablement lead* should **not** do?

- **A.** Preserve the field report in the enablement side's own systems with its full narrative and a timestamp, before passing anything to governance.
- **B.** Notify `head-of-ai-governance` and the SOC (plus privacy, since PII is involved) by named human using the rollout's escalation ladder.
- **C.** Interview the three operators directly to establish the sequence of events and draft a substantive write-up of what happened for the exec sponsor.
- **D.** Enter supporting mode — coordinate the champion-network response, operate the kill-switch and rollback plan if the incident owner requests it, and protect the communications cadence pending governance-side guidance.

## Answer key

**Q1 — C.** All five words are weak in the inherited program, but *manager-released* is the one with zero evidence: no written release paperwork exists at all. Chapter 01 names the written manager commitment as the single best predictor of the "crickets network" outcome, and its absence is what makes the other four words collapse under real workload pressure. (Chapter 01 — *What a champion IS, in one sentence* — the five load-bearing words and the role of the manager-release paperwork.)

**Q2 — B.** The evangelist-network smell is exactly this: outbound content carried faithfully, inbound signal sanitised to universally positive, incidents surface from elsewhere even though a champion was present. The cause Chapter 01 names is that the champion is being evaluated on adoption *up* rather than signal *in*. (Chapter 01 — *Three recognisable network failure shapes → The evangelist network*.)

**Q3 — C.** Chapter 02 lists the four channels in *decreasing order of signal quality* — team-manager nomination, existing-champion referral, open self-nomination with manager co-signature, HR/L&D data. The chapter's reasoning: nominations surface operators who are *already* doing the work informally; self-nomination skews enthusiast; HR data skews compliance-completer. (Chapter 02 — *Phase 1 — Recruit → Sourcing — four channels, in priority order*.)

**Q4 — C.** Chapter 02 names the three channels and says explicitly "the third channel is the one most programs underinvest in and is the one that actually retains champions beyond a first term." Attribution — "the AUP section 4 change came from reports by named champions in supply chain and legal ops" — is what makes the champion's work visible to them. (Chapter 02 — *Phase 3 — Recognise → The three recognition channels*.)

**Q5 — B.** Chapter 03 names this specific shape directly in Route 1's anti-pattern: "treating field reports as grievances to be acknowledged rather than as inputs to the backlog — 'Thank you for the feedback, we've noted it' without a ticket, without a route, without a date — that is the shape that breaks the loop within two quarters." Closing the loop at the record level is the single most important design decision in the chapter. (Chapter 03 — *Route 1 — into the policy backlog → The anti-pattern to avoid* and *Closing the loop at the record level*.)

**Q6 — D.** Chapter 03 walks this exact example in the cross-routing section. The policy-and-training coupling is explicit: "the policy change and the training change *must ship together* … the common failure is that the policy ships and the training does not; operators then learn the new policy from the old training material, which is worse than no policy change at all." Tooling does not route because the tool itself is fine; the gap is in the artifacts around it. (Chapter 03 — *The cross-routing cases*.)

**Q7 — B.** Chapter 04 says it directly: "flip (2) and (3) and the content reads like marketing." Reading outcomes before current state reverses the sequence an employee's eye follows and turns the cadence into a case-study deck. A and D are real failure patterns too but are not the specific "turns into marketing" swap the chapter names. (Chapter 04 — *The four slots, in order*.)

**Q8 — B.** Chapter 04 names the failed-attempt paragraph as one of three disciplines that keep the exec scorecard honest, and explicitly addresses the "boards want wins" pushback: "Boards and exec sponsors do not read this as weakness; they read it as evidence that the rest is honest. The alternative — all wins, every quarter — has a short half-life." The dissent slot more broadly is "the single element that differentiates reporting from marketing." (Chapter 04 — *The exec scorecard slot* and *The dissent slot*.)

**Q9 — C.** Chapter 05 names this pattern as the *soft mandate* — "a rollout that is announced as opt-in but enforced as a mandate — the operator who opts out gets a conversation with their manager two weeks later, or their performance metric for the quarter has quietly changed to include use of the tool. The organization reads this quickly; the trust cost is higher than a plain mandate would have been." The chapter frames it as the result of avoiding the decision rather than making it. (Chapter 05 — *The soft-mandate anti-pattern*.)

**Q10 — B.** Chapter 06's intervention for the "I already have ChatGPT at home" pattern is the three-move combination in B: reframe the training opening so it names what the session is and isn't, route experienced users into the specialist-tier fast-track, and build policy-first exercises. The ADKAR diagnosis is a Desire + misread-Knowledge-gap mismatch; forcing the foundational session confirms the operator's prior belief that the training is about things they already know. (Chapter 06 — *Pattern 3 — "I already have ChatGPT at home" → Interventions, specific*.)

**Q11 — C.** This is the first combined-signal read Chapter 07 names explicitly: "Adoption up, resistance survey quality-skepticism up → the silent-compliance risk is forming. Operators are using the tool but not trusting it. Intervene before the trust gap closes toward 'I'll use it badly and move on.'" The chapter's framing is that combined reads are the practice; single-signal reads are training wheels. (Chapter 07 — *The combined-signal reads that matter*.)

**Q12 — C.** Chapter 08 lists what the enablement lead explicitly *does not* do: conduct the forensic investigation, communicate with the operator about the incident substance (the governance side decides when and how), issue public statements ahead of the governance-side communications decision, or brief the exec sponsor on substance ahead of the governance side. A, B, and D are all parts of steps 1, 2, and 4 of the four-step handoff; C is the move the chapter names as a transfer-of-ownership violation. (Chapter 08 — *Point 2 — What "handoff" means operationally → Step 3 — Transfer the facts, not the ownership* and *Step 4 — Enter the enablement-side supporting mode*.)
