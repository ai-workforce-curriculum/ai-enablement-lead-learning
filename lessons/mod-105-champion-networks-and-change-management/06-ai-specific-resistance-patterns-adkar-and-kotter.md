# AI-Specific Resistance Patterns, Framed Through ADKAR and Kotter

> Generic change management is necessary but not sufficient. The workforce resistance patterns that attach to AI adoption have a specific shape — anxiety about personal job security, role-threat narratives that spread faster than policy clarifications, operators arriving with pre-existing ChatGPT habits from their personal lives, and a quality skepticism that is often empirically grounded. This chapter uses two established change-management frames — Prosci ADKAR and Kotter 8-Step — to diagnose each pattern and produce the specific intervention it needs.

## Why ADKAR and Kotter, and why not just them

Both frames predate the current AI adoption wave and have decades of evidence behind them. Using them signals to your change-management and HR partners that the enablement program speaks their vocabulary — which is a non-trivial partnership advantage, covered in [mod-104 Chapter 08](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/08-partnering-with-hr-ld-and-internal-comms.md).

What the frames give you:

- **ADKAR** (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) is the individual-level model. For each operator, resistance is a specific stage that stalled. The diagnostic question is "which letter is this person stuck at?" and the intervention follows.
- **Kotter 8-Step** is the organizational-level model. For a rollout, resistance is a systemic pattern in the organization. The diagnostic question is "which step did the rollout skip, under-invest in, or declare done too early?"

What they do not give you is anything AI-specific. The patterns in this chapter are the AI-specific adaptation — using the frames as the diagnostic grammar, not the content.

## Quick reference — the two frames

Both frames are treated at depth in their primary sources ([resources.md](resources.md)); the compact version below is for use inside this chapter.

### ADKAR, five stages

- **Awareness** — the operator knows the change is happening and understands why.
- **Desire** — the operator wants the change to succeed in their own context, which is different from understanding.
- **Knowledge** — the operator knows how to operate inside the change.
- **Ability** — the operator can, in practice, do the new thing in their actual workflow.
- **Reinforcement** — the organization reinforces the new behaviour until it becomes default.

Resistance is diagnosable by stage. An operator stuck at *Desire* is not helped by more training; an operator stuck at *Ability* is not helped by more communications.

### Kotter 8-Step, condensed

- 1. Establish urgency; 2. Build a guiding coalition; 3. Form the vision; 4. Communicate the vision; 5. Enable action by removing barriers; 6. Generate short-term wins; 7. Consolidate gains and produce more change; 8. Anchor the change in culture.

Resistance at the organizational level maps to the step skipped. A rollout where the "coalition" is one exec sponsor is a step-2 failure; a rollout with no short-term wins visible by month three is a step-6 failure.

The rest of this chapter maps the AI-specific patterns onto the two frames.

## Pattern 1 — AI anxiety

### What it looks like

An operator reports — in a survey, in an office hour, in a one-on-one with their manager — that the rollout feels like a threat they cannot name specifically. They describe sleep disruption, increased stress, worry about keeping up. The anxiety is not attached to a specific feature of the tool; it is attached to AI as a category. Scale this across a function and the function's adoption curve stalls even when the tool itself is fit for purpose.

AI anxiety is widely documented across major workforce surveys — Microsoft's Work Trend Index, LinkedIn's Workplace Learning Report, Deloitte's State of Generative AI, and the EU-OSHA workforce wellbeing reports have all surfaced the pattern since 2023. <!-- needs-research: refresh citations against the most recent editions of these surveys as of 2026 and cite specific prevalence figures. --> Treat the anxiety as predictable; design against it.

### ADKAR diagnosis

Usually Awareness is present (the operator knows AI is coming) and *Desire* has not formed. Pushing to Knowledge-stage interventions (more training) does not help an operator who has not yet moved past the emotional gate. A common misread is to treat anxiety as a Knowledge problem — "if we just teach them how it works, they'll be less afraid" — and respond with more technical material. The operator becomes more informed and remains anxious.

### Kotter diagnosis

Almost always a step-3 (vision) and step-5 (barriers) failure. The vision has not been personalised — the operator understands the organization's vision but not what it means for *their own* role. Barriers that would lower the anxiety (workload relief, career-path commitments, a credible explanation of what this change means for their role) have not been removed or named.

### Interventions, specific

- **At the organizational level (Kotter):** personalise the vision at the function level. The exec sponsor or the CAIO cannot calm an individual operator; the function leader naming what this means for *the function's* roles can. This is a Slot 1 and Slot 3 ([Chapter 04](04-the-rollout-communications-cadence.md)) content commitment, not a one-off memo.
- **At the individual level (ADKAR):** create space for the Desire stage. Facilitated conversations in the function — not training, not a town hall, a conversation — in which operators can name what they are worried about. The champion for the function hosts these; the enablement lead designs them but does not run every one. HR / L&D partnership from [mod-104 Chapter 08](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/08-partnering-with-hr-ld-and-internal-comms.md) is important here — these conversations are closer to L&D's existing expertise than to the CoE's.
- **What not to do:** publish a reassurance memo from the CEO that says "AI will augment, not replace, your work." The sentence is correct in many cases and credible in almost none from a leadership channel without a function-level commitment behind it. The commitment is what works; the sentence without the commitment is counter-productive.

### A note on genuine role threat

Some of what reads as anxiety is grounded in accurate perception. AI *is* reshaping some roles; some operators will see materially different work in two years. Pretending otherwise degrades the enablement program's credibility. The honest commitment the organization can make — and the champion network can help carry — is:

- *What roles are changing and over what timeframe.* The clearer the shape, the lower the anxiety-at-a-distance.
- *What support the organization is providing* for the operators whose roles are changing most — reskilling pathways, internal mobility channels, time for learning on the clock.
- *What is not being asked* — the organization is not asking operators to be AI engineers, prompt engineers, or vendor-certified builders. The literacy floor is the floor ([mod-101 Chapter 05](../mod-101-role-and-the-org-wide-enablement-operating-model/05-eu-ai-act-article-4-and-the-literacy-floor.md)).

## Pattern 2 — Role-threat narratives

### What it looks like

A narrative travels through the function faster than any policy clarification does: *"They're rolling out this tool to replace us."* The narrative often has a specific proximate trigger — a layoff elsewhere in the industry, a vendor demo that over-claimed autonomy, a leader's off-hand comment. The narrative is sticky because it is simple and emotionally charged.

The narrative is different from genuine role-threat (above): it is a *story* that may or may not match the actual roadmap. But because resistance is about perception, the narrative matters whether or not it is accurate.

### ADKAR diagnosis

An *Awareness* failure — the operator has become aware of a story, not of the change. The story is driving Desire into negative territory. Attempts to push forward (more Knowledge, more Ability content) are rejected as "tools of the change I don't want."

### Kotter diagnosis

Step-4 (communicate the vision) failure under competition. The organization's vision for the change is not the only vision circulating; a competing narrative has filled the vacuum. Kotter's model emphasises that step-4 communications must happen at an order of magnitude more than leadership typically plans — the narrative advantage goes to whichever story is told most often.

### Interventions, specific

- **Name the narrative explicitly.** The communications cadence names the narrative in Slot 1 or Slot 3 — not defensively, not by denying it, but by addressing it directly: "Some are hearing this rollout as a replacement plan. It is not; here is what it is, here is what the org has committed to, here is what we are not asking of you." This is uncomfortable to write and more effective than silence.
- **Champion-carried counter-narratives, not centre-authored memos.** Narratives beat memos in every culture. A champion's spotlighted story about how their own workflow changed — specifically, not vaguely — carries more weight than any executive comms. The use-case library and spotlight program ([mod-104 Chapter 05](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/05-the-delivery-mix.md)) is the primary tool.
- **Show the failed-attempt paragraph in the cadence.** A program that admits, publicly, that a pilot was killed or a tool did not work is read as honest. Operators who read the cadence as honest are less receptive to the competing narrative.
- **Match the shape of the commitment to the shape of the narrative.** If the narrative is "we will be replaced," the counter must be about individual role-security commitments, not aggregate productivity gains. Mismatched responses are heard as deflection.

## Pattern 3 — "I already have ChatGPT at home"

### What it looks like

An operator — often a technically confident one — arrives at a function rollout saying some version of "I've been using ChatGPT at home for a year, I don't need your training." The operator may be exactly right about their personal skill and exactly wrong about why your training exists. The pattern often appears in engineering, product, design, and sales functions first; it spreads.

The pattern is a mix of pre-existing fluency (genuine) and a misreading of the training's purpose (resolvable). The training is rarely about how to prompt; it is about *how to prompt in this organization's context* — data-handling boundaries, approved-tool envelope, escalation paths, policy awareness.

### ADKAR diagnosis

A *Desire* and *Knowledge* mismatch. The operator has high Knowledge of consumer AI; they lack Knowledge of the organizational context; they have no Desire to invest in the gap because they do not see the gap. Pushing more generic training content makes the mismatch worse — the content reinforces the operator's view that the training is about things they already know.

### Kotter diagnosis

Step-3 (vision) failure. The vision has been under-differentiated between consumer AI and enterprise AI; the operator reads them as the same thing because the organization has not drawn the line.

### Interventions, specific

- **Rework the training opening.** The first ten minutes of the function's workshop names, explicitly, what the training *is* and *is not*. "You know how to prompt. We know you know how to prompt. The next three hours is about the policy context, the data-handling boundaries, the escalation path, the use-case patterns your function has agreed on. Your consumer skill is a starting point — this is the organizational context around it."
- **Create a specialist-tier fast-track.** The ChatGPT-at-home operator is often a specialist-tier candidate ([mod-104 Chapter 02](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/02-audience-segmentation-and-fluency-targets.md)) in waiting. Route them into the specialist curriculum rather than making them sit through foundational content a second time. This also fills the champion pipeline ([Chapter 02](02-the-four-phase-lifecycle-recruit-train-recognise-retire.md)).
- **Build a policy-first exercise.** A hands-on exercise that an experienced personal-use operator will *also* learn from — e.g., "here's a workflow that works well with ChatGPT at home; now walk through what changes when the data is customer records, the policy is this organization's AUP, and the escalation path is our ticketing system." This moves the operator from Desire-resistance into Knowledge engagement.
- **What not to do:** make the training condescending to experienced users. A reputation for condescension travels further than a reputation for being essential.

### The shadow-IT edge case

Some "I have ChatGPT at home" operators are already using personal ChatGPT accounts for work tasks — pasting internal documents into consumer accounts, uploading customer data. This is a shadow-IT and data-handling problem that the champion network will surface via [Chapter 03](03-routing-field-signal-back-to-policy-tooling-and-training.md). The resistance pattern and the shadow-IT problem are related but not identical; treat the shadow-IT path through the policy backlog, not through the resistance intervention.

## Pattern 4 — Quality skepticism

### What it looks like

An operator reports that the AI output is wrong, unreliable, or lower-quality than their own work. The report may be accurate (the tool is making errors in the specific workflow), partly accurate (the tool is good at some tasks and bad at others, and the operator has over-generalised from one failure), or inaccurate (the tool is performing well and the operator's standard is different from the measured one).

This is the pattern most likely to be empirically grounded. AI tools do make mistakes; vendor marketing routinely over-promises; workflows that work in a demo often break under real data. A skeptical operator is often a useful signal.

### ADKAR diagnosis

An *Ability* or *Reinforcement* failure that presents as resistance. The operator may have Knowledge but has not developed the verification habits that make the Ability stage work with a probabilistic tool. The resistance is in effect "I tried it, it didn't work, I stopped."

### Kotter diagnosis

Step-6 (short-term wins) failure. The rollout has not produced visible, credible wins from inside the function that the skeptical operator can read as evidence. The skeptic's experience has not been counterbalanced by signal from peers they trust.

### Interventions, specific

- **Take the skepticism seriously as signal.** The first move is to route the specific failure through the field-report loop ([Chapter 03](03-routing-field-signal-back-to-policy-tooling-and-training.md)) — is this a real failure mode of the tool, a workflow mismatch, an unmet training gap, or an operator-level preference? Each has a different intervention.
- **Teach verification as a first-class skill.** The role-based playbook ([mod-104 Chapter 04](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/04-role-based-playbooks-and-use-case-libraries.md)) names, for each use case, the verification step — the spot-check, the citation back to source, the colleague review. A skeptical operator trained in verification is often the function's strongest adopter within one cycle, because their standards are higher and their verified outputs are trusted.
- **Bring the skeptic into the use-case library authoring.** A skeptic who contributes a use case gains visible investment in the program; the use case they contribute is often better than an enthusiast's because it has been stress-tested.
- **Do not argue with quality claims using vendor metrics.** Vendor benchmarks do not land on an operator looking at their own work. The counter has to be peer-produced from inside the function.

### The cost of dismissing it

Quality skepticism dismissed as resistance becomes incident risk. An operator who reports that the tool is unreliable, is not listened to, and complies anyway is the operator whose workflow quietly degrades until an incident surfaces the issue. The skeptic was the signal the program ignored.

## Pattern 5 — The silent compliance pattern

A brief fifth pattern, worth naming because it is easy to miss.

### What it looks like

The rollout is mandated; the training is completed on schedule; usage metrics look good; satisfaction surveys come back neutral-to-positive; nothing breaks. The scorecard reads healthy. Six months in, operator behaviour reverts to pre-rollout patterns in all but the most visible surface use. The mandate was complied with; the change was not adopted.

### ADKAR diagnosis

*Reinforcement* failure under mandate. The organization got through Awareness-Desire-Knowledge-Ability on the strength of the mandate, and did not invest in Reinforcement — the day-to-day signals that the new behaviour is the expected behaviour. Without reinforcement, mandated behaviour has a half-life.

### Kotter diagnosis

Step-7 (consolidate gains) and step-8 (anchor in culture) failure. The rollout was treated as a project with an end date rather than as a change that needed anchoring.

### Interventions, specific

- **Instrument behaviour, not just use.** Scorecard metrics should distinguish active use from compliance clicks ([mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md)).
- **Treat the first 12 months as the program, not the first 90 days.** The communications cadence from [Chapter 04](04-the-rollout-communications-cadence.md) runs long past launch for exactly this reason.
- **Champion-led reinforcement rituals.** The function's champion runs a monthly "what are we doing differently now" check-in — not a training, a retrospective. The ritual is what moves the mandate through to anchor.

## A mapping table for the diagnostic walk

| Pattern | ADKAR stage stuck | Kotter step under-invested | Primary intervention |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI anxiety | Desire | 3 (vision), 5 (barriers) | Function-level vision personalisation; facilitated conversation; named commitments |
| Role-threat narrative | Awareness distorted by competing narrative | 4 (communicate the vision) | Name the narrative explicitly; champion-carried counter-stories; honest cadence |
| "I have ChatGPT at home" | Desire + misread Knowledge gap | 3 (vision under-differentiated) | Reframe training opening; specialist fast-track; policy-first exercise |
| Quality skepticism | Ability / Reinforcement | 6 (short-term wins) | Treat as signal; verification as first-class skill; bring skeptic into authoring |
| Silent compliance | Reinforcement | 7 and 8 | Instrument behaviour; long cadence; champion-led rituals |

The diagnostic walk — *which pattern is this? which stage is stuck? which step was under-invested?* — is the move the chapter is teaching. The specific intervention follows.

## Partnership with HR / L&D on resistance work

Resistance work lives in overlap territory between the CoE and HR / L&D. The partnership contract from [mod-104 Chapter 08](../mod-104-training-programs-and-ai-literacy-curriculum/08-partnering-with-hr-ld-and-internal-comms.md) is what prevents duplication and gap. The slice that applies here:

- Facilitated conversations (AI anxiety, pattern 1) are co-run — L&D facilitates, enablement provides the content frame and the function-specific context.
- Role-threat work (pattern 2) involves HR directly where organizational role changes are being discussed — the enablement lead carries the communications side, HR carries the role-redesign side.
- ADKAR and Kotter vocabulary is almost certainly already in HR / L&D use in your organization. Speak their vocabulary; do not rebuild it.

## Summary

- Generic change management is necessary but not sufficient; AI adoption has specific resistance patterns that need named interventions.
- Use ADKAR as the individual-level diagnostic and Kotter as the organizational-level diagnostic; they are complementary, not competing.
- Five patterns: AI anxiety, role-threat narratives, "I have ChatGPT at home," quality skepticism, silent compliance. Each has a stage-stuck signature and a specific intervention.
- Quality skepticism is the pattern most likely to be empirically grounded; treat it as signal first, resistance second. Dismissed skepticism becomes incident risk.
- Resistance work is co-owned with HR / L&D; speak their ADKAR / Kotter vocabulary rather than rebuilding the frames.
- Exercise 05 produces a resistance playbook grounded in the five patterns and the two frames.
