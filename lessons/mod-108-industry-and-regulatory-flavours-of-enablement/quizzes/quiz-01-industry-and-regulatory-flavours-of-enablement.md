# Quiz 01 — Industry and Regulatory Flavours of Enablement

**Format:** multiple choice · **Questions:** 10 · **Estimated effort:** 22–28 minutes
**Grounding:** [Chapter 01](../01-the-industry-flavour-problem.md), [Chapter 02](../02-eu-ai-act-article-4-training-content-at-depth.md), [Chapter 03](../03-healthcare-flavour-hipaa-and-phi.md), [Chapter 04](../04-finance-flavour-sr-11-7-and-finra.md), [Chapter 05](../05-legal-engineering-flavour.md), [Chapter 06](../06-education-flavour-ferpa-coppa-and-ed-tech.md), [Chapter 07](../07-enablement-vs-governance-responsibility-split.md), [Chapter 08](../08-partnering-with-industry-counsel-without-opining.md)

## How to take this quiz

Pick the single best answer for each question. Each question tests whether you can *apply* a chapter idea to a situation — paraphrasing the chapter is not enough. The answer key with short explanations is at the bottom; resist reading it until you have your own answers written down.

### Q1. An enablement lead joins a 10,000-person US-headquartered bank from a 4,000-person SaaS company. On week one, they port their SaaS AUP into the bank's template, substituting the bank's name and tool list. Governance signs it quickly; the model risk team flags concerns six weeks later when a credit analyst asks about using the approved coding assistant on an ECOA-touching workflow. Under Chapter 01, the primary diagnostic the enablement lead should run on their own week-one move — and the correct frame for the fix — is:

- **A.** The port failed because the bank's template was out of date; the fix is a template refresh with governance.
- **B.** The port failed because the industry-neutral artifact's data classes ("confidential," "personal") did not map to the bank's regulatory data taxonomy (NPI, MNPI) and the approved-use list did not survive a regulator's reading against SR 11-7; the fix is not to re-author alone but to invite the flavour-aware counterparts (MRM, CCO, fair-lending officer) into co-authoring from the first draft — the role is portable, the artifacts are not.
- **C.** The port was fine; the failure was in change-management with the credit analyst population and should be addressed by an AUP roadshow.
- **D.** The port failed because the SaaS AUP's prohibitions were too permissive; tightening the prohibited-uses list alone will close the gap.

### Q2. An enablement lead at a 6,000-person US-headquartered SaaS company with a 400-person EU subsidiary is asked by legal whether the organization needs an Article 4 program. The lead's four scope decisions are: contractors (yes), outsourced operators (yes), US-only employees whose AI-assisted output can reach EU customers (**no** — "we read territoriality narrowly"), new hires (from day 30, not day one — "we onboard HR content first"). Under Chapter 02, which decisions are defensible and which are the specific failure modes the chapter names?

- **A.** All four are defensible; Article 4 explicitly permits narrow territorial reads.
- **B.** The two "yes" decisions are correct; the US-only "no" and the day-30 new-hire decision are both specific failure modes — the chapter's default when in doubt is to *scope wider* (a narrow territorial read is cheap to adopt and expensive to defend against a market-surveillance authority), and the at-launch posture ("every in-scope person has completed the relevant tier before they operate an AI tool against EU-touching activity, including new hires before production use") is the only defensible shape — day-30 is backfill posture.
- **C.** Only the new-hire decision is wrong; the territorial read is fine if documented.
- **D.** Only the US-only decision is wrong; new hires at day 30 is a reasonable operating accommodation.

### Q3. An enablement lead publishes a content-update trigger matrix with seven rows (envelope change, AUP/SOP revision, EU AI Office guidance, incident finding, enforcement action, vendor material change, annual methodology review). The matrix shows review windows in calendar days but the "owner" column is marked "TBD" across every row because the governance council has not yet formally assigned them, and the enablement lead has not yet pushed. Under Chapter 02, the defect this creates and the correct fix are:

- **A.** No defect; owners can be assigned after the first trigger fires, since the matrix is still new.
- **B.** The defect is cosmetic; the fix is to write "enablement lead" into every row for consistency.
- **C.** Without named owners, the triggers do not fire — the matrix is the written rule and the named-owner column is what makes it operational; the fix is to name a *role* owner per row (not a person, because people turn over) — e.g., the approved-tool envelope owner surfaces envelope changes to curriculum on commit, the AUP owner routes revisions on redline-finalization, the incident lead surfaces findings on close — and the scorecard should publish the review-window compliance as a live signal.
- **D.** The matrix should be deprecated in favour of a quarterly manual walkthrough; trigger matrices do not survive contact with real content-update cycles.

### Q4. A clinical-informatics team at a healthcare organization has started using an approved coding assistant (BAA on file) to draft patient-portal replies that summarize visit notes. Volume is growing; the privacy officer flags it for review. The enablement lead's inclination is to write a short memo stating that under HIPAA's minimum necessary standard the use is permitted, because the tool has a BAA and operators are only providing minimum PHI per prompt. Under Chapters 03 and 08, this is the wrong move. The right move — and the reason the difference matters — is:

- **A.** The memo is fine; the enablement lead has the operational context closest to the use. Co-sign with the privacy officer at the end.
- **B.** The memo should go to the privacy officer as a recommendation; if they do not respond in 14 days, the use is auto-approved.
- **C.** Write a one-page situation memo that includes the use pattern with example prompts and outputs, the BAA terms, operator population and volume, proposed guardrails, and the specific question for the privacy officer — "does this use meet HIPAA's minimum necessary and the BAA's defined services?" — with timing signals and a paused-pending-response posture. The privacy officer makes the regulatory determination; the enablement lead executes against it. The operational substance is the same in both moves; the difference is *who holds the determination* — and under Chapter 08, a regulatory conclusion written by a non-licensed operator loses the professional-responsibility protections that attach when the privacy officer (the HIPAA-required named role) decides.
- **D.** Escalate the matter to the general counsel for a privilege opinion before the privacy officer sees it.

### Q5. Three bank intakes land on the enablement lead's desk in one week: (1) a GenAI drafter for internal marketing copy that is reviewed by the CMO's team; (2) a tool that scores candidate credit applications and surfaces a recommendation to the analyst; (3) a chat assistant that summarizes call-center transcripts. Compliance asks the enablement lead for a quick "is it a model under SR 11-7?" call on each so they can plan the MRM queue. Under Chapter 04 (and the Chapter 07 three-lines-of-defense test), the correct response is:

- **A.** Call tool (2) a model and the other two not models; email compliance within the day.
- **B.** Decline to classify any of the three; route all three to MRM via the intake questionnaire with the three sub-questions filled in (does the output affect a decision/estimate/quantitative judgment? does it materially influence business or regulatory outcomes — credit, suitability, pricing, reserving, compliance? is the output used in any way that would need to be defended to a regulator?) — the enablement lead carries the *routing* discipline, not the determination; the "is it a model?" call is MRM's, and making it on the fly while MRM is slow is the specific drift Chapter 07 names as enablement-absorbing-governance.
- **C.** Classify (2) as a model and route it to MRM; approve (1) and (3) as non-regulated.
- **D.** Pause all three until MRM assigns a liaison to the enablement intake queue.

### Q6. A legal-engineering enablement lead at a media conglomerate — an attorney themselves, admitted in two states — is pulled into a discussion with practice-area managing counsel about whether a specific self-hosted model deployment preserves attorney-client privilege for a specific M&A matter. Counsel asks for the lead's view because the lead holds the bar card. Under Chapters 05, 07, and 08, which response is in scope?

- **A.** Give the view directly — the lead is licensed and the practice-area counsel has asked; this is the efficient path and the lead is accountable for their own professional-responsibility calls.
- **B.** Decline to answer on the ground that privilege questions are always outside enablement scope, without separating hats.
- **C.** Separate the hats explicitly ("speaking as a licensed [profession] I would…" versus "speaking as the enablement lead"); on this call the right move is almost always to route the specific privilege determination to the general counsel's office — the GC holds the privilege theory and the state-bar-facing posture under Chapter 07's split — and to keep the written artifacts (AUP, SOP, matter-classification SOP) on the enablement side carrying the *operational substance* (self-hosted configuration, no-logging posture, dedicated tenant, matter-level classification) that lets counsel's theory hold up, without the written artifacts carrying professional opinion.
- **D.** Treat the dual role as the organization's efficient answer: the lead owns both the enablement artifacts and the privilege determinations for the legal function, since the role is co-located with legal.

### Q7. An ed-tech vendor's enablement lead is building the workforce AUP. Customer success has asked for permission to use the internal chat assistant on redacted samples of "learning-analytics telemetry" (clickstream, time-on-task, engagement signals) from one large district customer, to help them reproduce a support issue. The vendor's customer contract with the district is silent on learning analytics specifically. Under Chapter 06 and Chapter 07, the right move is:

- **A.** Approve — learning-analytics data is not FERPA-defined and state law has not caught up; the ambiguity defaults to the vendor's internal use.
- **B.** Approve subject to the general-workforce "no customer data" line; if customer success thinks it is useful, the risk is on their side.
- **C.** Treat learning-analytics data as a named class in the AUP (Chapter 06 explicitly flags it as often the most sensitive class in an AI-enabled product and the one state laws have increasingly named explicitly); apply the SOP's customer-contract-alignment rule (the strictest applicable customer contract governs) and route the contract-silent question to legal before any use; route the product-vs-workforce boundary question to the chief privacy officer per the Chapter 07 split (the CPO owns the product's privacy posture; the enablement lead owns the workforce surface). Default to no until legal and the CPO sign off — not auto-approve on silence.
- **D.** Treat the request as a product-privacy matter and remove it from the enablement intake entirely.

### Q8. An EU member-state market-surveillance authority writes to the organization: *"Who in your organization is accountable for Article 4 compliance?"* The enablement lead drafts the response. Under Chapter 07, the defensible shape of the reply is:

- **A.** A single name — the enablement lead — since the enablement lead operates the curriculum, the trigger matrix, and the scope-definition record.
- **B.** A single name — the head of AI governance — since Article 4 is a regulator-facing compliance posture.
- **C.** A two-named answer: `head-of-ai-governance` is accountable for the AI Act compliance posture at the organization level (including the classification of systems under the AI Act's risk tiers and the regulator-engagement with EU authorities); the AI Enablement Lead is accountable for the operational substance that makes the posture defensible — the literacy curriculum content for every tier, the content-update trigger matrix and its running operation, and the scope-definition record as a co-signed artifact. A single-named answer is either wrong (enablement-lead isolation) or incomplete (governance-lead isolation).
- **D.** Decline to name anyone until counsel has reviewed under legal privilege.

### Q9. The enablement lead at a PBM (pharmacy benefits manager, operating as a HIPAA business associate) is asked by HR to "run the AI-and-HIPAA training" for the full workforce — HR's reasoning is that the enablement lead already has training infrastructure stood up and the privacy officer's team is three people. Under Chapters 03 and 07, the correct posture is:

- **A.** Accept. Enablement has the capacity; the privacy officer's team can focus on breach assessment. The HIPAA training program is a training-ops matter.
- **B.** Accept temporarily — "90-day loan" — and plan to hand back to privacy later.
- **C.** Decline to absorb the HIPAA training program; it belongs to the privacy officer or compliance officer under the HIPAA Privacy Rule §164.530(b) and Security Rule §164.308(a)(5) workforce-training obligations. The move is to *integrate* the AI-literacy content into the HIPAA training program (shared roster, aligned refresh cadence, coupled triggers, co-signed integration note) while leaving the HIPAA training program itself under the privacy officer. Surface the privacy officer's capacity gap as a scorecard signal to leadership; do not backfill it quietly. Absorbing the whole HIPAA training program is the specific enablement-absorbs-governance failure mode the healthcare chapter names.
- **D.** Refer HR to the compliance officer and disengage entirely from the question.

### Q10. In a Thursday meeting with the chief compliance officer at a broker-dealer, an enablement lead is asked whether a proposed AI-assisted drafting workflow for registered representatives falls under FINRA Rule 3110 supervision. Trying to be useful and move quickly, the lead says: *"Under Rule 3110, the correct approach is supervisory pre-review of any AI-generated customer communication; the precedent on this is FINRA Notice 24-09, and I'd read the regulatory requirement as pre-review at the registered-principal level."* Under Chapter 08, which sentence patterns have drifted — and what is the in-scope redraft?

- **A.** No drift — the enablement lead is being efficient and the CCO is the right audience for the direct view.
- **B.** Only "precedent on this is…" has drifted; the rest is fine.
- **C.** Every sentence has drifted: "Under [rule], the correct approach is…" is a legal conclusion; "precedent on this is…" is a precedent-cite (a legal act); "I'd read the regulatory requirement as…" is interpretation of a regulatory requirement (also a legal act). The in-scope redraft uses operator-surfaces-a-question patterns: *"Operationally, we see this workflow being used by [N] registered representatives on an estimated [X per week] customer communications. The training module and SOP would need to say either (a) pre-review by a registered principal, (b) post-send sampled supervision, or (c) something between — which is correct under compliance's reading of Rule 3110 and Notice 24-09? I can draft operator-facing language against the compliance position; the position is compliance's. If the decision changes the approved-tool envelope, I will coordinate."*
- **D.** The drift is only in tone; swapping "I'd read" for "it seems to me" fixes the sentence.

## Answer key

**Q1 — B.** Chapter 01 names three recurring failure modes when an industry-neutral artifact walks into a regulated industry: data-class mismatch (the generic "confidential"/"personal" taxonomy does not map to NPI/MNPI/PHI/privilege), approved-use overreach (the generic "generate code for production features" does not survive SR 11-7's reading of regulated-decision workflows), and missing regulatory evidence on the scorecard. The chapter is explicit: the fix is co-authoring with flavour-aware counterparts from the first draft, not re-authoring alone — the role is portable across flavours, the artifacts are not. A, C, and D each miss the root cause. (Chapter 01 — *Why the industry-neutral playbook breaks on contact*.)

**Q2 — B.** Chapter 02 is explicit on both decisions. On scope — the default when in doubt is to scope wider: "best extent" is read against the organization's actual AI surface, not the smallest scope the literal text permits, and a narrow-scope posture backed by a theory read of territoriality is one a reasonably sophisticated market-surveillance authority will push back on. On timing — the at-launch posture is the only defensible shape; new-hire onboarding includes the relevant tier's content before production use, and backfill-after-the-fact is the posture most organizations are in and that has been dragging but is not defensible against an active inquiry. (Chapter 02 — *The scope decision* and *The "by when" question*.)

**Q3 — C.** Chapter 02 names the three disciplines that make the trigger matrix survive contact: each trigger has a named owner (otherwise the triggers do not fire), the review window is a deadline not an aspiration (publish compliance in the scorecard), and the matrix itself is versioned. The chapter is specific that owners are role names so the matrix survives staffing changes, and gives the concrete examples (envelope owner surfaces changes on commit; AUP owner routes revisions on redline-finalization; incident lead surfaces findings on close). (Chapter 02 — *The content-update trigger matrix*.)

**Q4 — C.** Chapter 08's healthcare case walk names this exact pattern. The wrong move is a memo stating the use is permitted under HIPAA's minimum necessary standard — that is the enablement lead issuing a regulatory conclusion. The right move is a situation memo that is identical in operational substance but frames a specific decision for the privacy officer (the HIPAA-required named role under 45 CFR 164.530) with timing signals and a paused-pending-response posture. The deeper reason Chapter 08 gives: professional-responsibility rules bind the people who can give opinions; when a non-licensed operator writes the opinion, the protections silently do not attach — a harm the organization finds out about on the worst possible timing. (Chapter 08 — *A short case walk — healthcare example* and *Why this posture is load-bearing*; Chapter 03 — *The drifts to watch*.)

**Q5 — B.** Chapter 04 names "is it a model?" as the single question the bank enablement lead will be asked most often and is most expected to have a clear routing answer to — and names that the answer is almost never the enablement lead's to give. The chapter's three sub-questions (output affects a decision/estimate/judgment; materially influences business or regulatory outcomes; would need to be defended to a regulator) are the *routing* structure the enablement lead carries into the intake questionnaire, not the determination. Chapter 07's finance overlay is explicit that making classification calls on the fly because MRM is slow is the enablement-absorbs-governance failure mode; the move is to escalate MRM latency to the CRO as a scorecard signal, not to substitute enablement judgment for MRM determination. (Chapter 04 — *The "is it a model?" question*; Chapter 07 — *The industry-specific overlay — finance*.)

**Q6 — C.** Chapter 08's section on dual-hatted licensed professionals names the two disciplines: separate the hats explicitly in speech, and keep written artifacts on the enablement side carrying operational substance only, not professional opinion — "written artifacts are the enablement lead's; opinions are the licensed professional's — and the organization typically expects the licensed role, not the enablement role, to be the source." Chapter 07's legal-engineering overlay names the privilege theory as governance's (specifically the general counsel's) — when wearing the enablement hat, route professional-responsibility questions to the GC or managing counsel, even when the enablement lead has the credential to answer them personally. Chapter 05 reinforces that privilege is an attorney's call and the GC's office owns the theory; the enablement lead writes the operational substance that lets the theory hold up. (Chapter 08 — *When the enablement lead is themselves a licensed professional*; Chapter 07 — *The industry-specific overlay — legal engineering*; Chapter 05 — *The drifts to watch*.)

**Q7 — C.** Chapter 06 is explicit that learning-analytics / behavioral data (clickstream, time-on-task, engagement signals) is often the most sensitive class in an AI-enabled product and the least well-controlled, and that state laws have increasingly begun to name this class explicitly. The ed-tech SOP's customer-contract-alignment rule governs: when the workforce uses an AI tool on data covered by a customer contract, the tool and workflow must comply with the strictest applicable customer contract. Chapter 07's vendor-side split places product privacy with the chief privacy officer and the workforce surface with the enablement lead — a product/workforce overlap of this shape is surfaced to the CPO for a formal boundary, not quietly absorbed. Contract silence does not default to approval. (Chapter 06 — *The data classes that attach* and *Ed-tech vendor-side deltas*; Chapter 07 — *At the ed-tech vendor*.)

**Q8 — C.** Chapter 07 writes exactly this split for Article 4 and calls out the market-surveillance authority question explicitly: the honest answer is a two-named answer — the head of AI governance is accountable for the compliance posture; the enablement lead is accountable for the operational substance (curriculum content, content-update trigger matrix, scope-definition record as co-signed artifact). "A single-named answer is either wrong (enablement lead isolation) or incomplete (governance lead isolation)." (Chapter 07 — *The EU AI Act Article 4 split, revisited*.)

**Q9 — C.** Chapter 03 names this exact drift ("You're the HIPAA trainer") and the exact move: the HIPAA training program is the privacy officer's or compliance officer's under the Privacy Rule's workforce-training obligation at §164.530(b) and the Security Rule's awareness-and-training standard at §164.308(a)(5); the AI-literacy program *integrates with* it (shared roster, aligned refresh cadence, coupled triggers) but is not absorbed into it. Chapter 07's healthcare overlay reinforces that absorbing the whole HIPAA training program is the specific enablement-absorbs-governance failure mode, and that the fix is to surface the capacity gap to leadership, not backfill it quietly. (Chapter 03 — *The drifts to watch*; Chapter 07 — *The industry-specific overlay — healthcare*.)

**Q10 — C.** Chapter 08's "starting sentences that have drifted" list names each of these patterns directly: *"Under [rule], the correct approach is…"*, *"The precedent on this is…"* ("precedent-citing is a legal act"), and *"The regulatory requirement is…"* ("interpreting the regulatory requirement is a legal act; summarizing a confirmed requirement is fine"). The in-scope redraft uses the operator-surfaces-a-question posture: operational situation, concrete question framed as a decision for compliance, structure for the answer (not a proposed answer), timing signal, and the closing commitment to carry the decision into the AUP/SOP/training once compliance returns it. The redraft is the same substance; the ownership of the regulatory determination is different. (Chapter 08 — *The sentence patterns that stay in scope*, *The three postures to know by name — Posture 1*, *The four questions that signal drift*.)
