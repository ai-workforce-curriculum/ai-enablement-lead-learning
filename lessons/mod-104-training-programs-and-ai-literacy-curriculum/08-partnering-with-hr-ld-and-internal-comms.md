# Partnering with HR, L&D, and Internal Comms

> You are not going to deliver this program alone. Most of the hours that get the curriculum into operators' heads will be delivered by people who do not report to you — the L&D organization, the HR business partners, the internal comms team, the champion network, the team managers. The failure mode is treating these partners as resources to be consumed. The posture is partnership — a written contract, named owners, named handoffs, and clear boundaries that stay stable through budget cycles, re-orgs, and personnel turnover. This chapter is the partnership design.

## Four partners, four distinct contributions

Each partner brings something the enablement lead's team cannot source alone. Name the contribution before you name the ask.

### Learning & Development

What they bring:

- Industrial-strength instructional design. The CoE's curriculum content is subject-matter-expert first; L&D makes it learnable.
- LMS ownership. The system of record for completion, assessment, and compliance evidence (Layer 1 from [Chapter 07](07-measuring-fluency-growth.md)) lives in L&D's tenancy.
- The vendor and seat-license purchasing relationships for commercial learning content.
- The organization's talent-development frameworks (often ATD-based) that the AI literacy curriculum has to interoperate with.

What you bring to them:

- The subject-matter authority on AI content — they are not going to re-author the policy-into-practice modules.
- The regulatory clarity on Article 4 obligations and the evidence shape.
- The approved-tool envelope and its freshness — their course catalog assumes stable tools; yours does not.

Where the friction happens: L&D runs on an annual calendar; AI tools do not. L&D's courseware refresh cycles are measured in quarters; your approved-tool envelope changes weekly. The partnership contract needs to acknowledge this directly and carve out faster-moving channels (self-serve, spotlights, lunch-and-learns) that do not live inside the LMS-bound cycle.

### Human Resources (and HR business partners)

What they bring:

- The in-scope population data. Who is in each function, who is a contractor, who sits in which jurisdiction, who is on leave. You cannot roster the training without this.
- The job-architecture and leveling data that maps people to the fluency tiers.
- The acceptable-use enforcement path when operators refuse the training or breach the policy.
- The onboarding and offboarding pathways where new hires enter the curriculum and departing people are closed out.

What you bring to them:

- The AI-specific overlay on the jobs they are architecting. "Which roles require AI literacy at the specialist tier" is your input to their job description.
- The data on which roles are shifting toward AI-dense work (feeds their workforce planning).
- The incident signal when AI misuse becomes a people problem.

Where the friction happens: HR's primary posture is confidentiality; the enablement team's posture is transparency. The contract carves out which data flows which way, and when.

### Internal Comms

What they bring:

- The organization's narrative. The all-hands slot, the intranet front page, the executive town hall, the newsletter, the Slack/Teams announcement channel.
- Editorial standards — what the organization's internal voice is, what gets through editorial review, what does not.
- Audience-targeting capability — the ability to reach specific populations (the service team only, the EMEA region only, the leadership tier only) without blasting everyone.

What you bring to them:

- Real use-case material for spotlights ([Chapter 05](05-the-delivery-mix.md)).
- Timing on when AI policy or tool changes need to go out organization-wide.
- Executive scorecard material for leadership comms moments.

Where the friction happens: internal comms is cautious with uncertain or evolving stories; AI tooling is uncertain and evolving. Negotiate a *review pipeline* that moves faster than the default editorial cycle for AI-relevant announcements — and a short-list of recurring formats (the use-case spotlight, the monthly AI update) that have pre-cleared templates.

### The Champion Network

Formally treated in [mod-105](../mod-105-champion-networks-and-change-management/README.md); mentioned here because for training-program delivery, champions carry a large share of the role-based playbook rollout, the office hours, the coaching clinics, and the use-case spotlight sourcing.

What they bring: credibility inside their function, local context, time budgeted (by their manager) for the champion role.

What you bring: content, coaching curriculum, recognition, career signal, the connection to the broader program.

The champion network is partner-shaped but not peer-shaped — it is *your* network, run from the CoE, with its own charter. Keep the operating distinction clean; running the champion network like a federation of indie contractors produces inconsistency and overload.

## The deferral: team-scope delivery to `ai-augmented-team-manager`

The most important internal-to-the-organization deferral in this module runs downward. Team-scope delivery — the one team's specific workshop, the one team's playbook rollout, the one team's AI standup — belongs to the team manager, not to the enablement lead's team.

[`ai-augmented-team-manager`](https://github.com/ai-workforce-curriculum/ai-augmented-team-manager-learning) at level 40 is the role that owns team-scope AI adoption. The enablement lead's program equips that role; the enablement lead's program does not substitute for it.

The handoff is:

- The enablement lead publishes the role-based playbook, the prompt library, the facilitator kit, and the measurement rubrics.
- The team manager consumes the kit and delivers it to their own team on their own cadence.
- The enablement lead's team supports — through office hours, champion routing, coaching — but does not run the team's weekly rituals.

Common drift: the enablement lead's team is drawn into running one or two teams' weekly adoption rituals because those teams are high-visibility. Every hour spent there is an hour not spent on the organization-wide program. Hold the line; route high-visibility teams to their own manager's playbook delivery with support, not substitution.

The team manager's curriculum has its own measurement stack, its own cadence, its own recognition path. Do not re-author their work from the enablement side.

## The partnership contract — one artifact

The artifact of this chapter is the **partnership contract** — one document, three to five pages, co-signed by the enablement lead and the counterpart lead from each partner function. One contract per partner. Four contracts total in the first year (L&D, HR, Internal Comms, and the champion network's charter — the last of which is formally in [mod-105](../mod-105-champion-networks-and-change-management/README.md)).

Each contract has five sections:

1. **Shared goal** — a sentence or two naming what the partnership is for. Not platitudes. "Deliver the AI literacy foundational curriculum to the in-scope population with Article 4-defensible evidence, on an annual refresh cadence" is a shared goal. "Build AI culture together" is not.
2. **Roles and responsibilities** — a short table: who authors, who reviews, who delivers, who maintains the evidence, who takes the audit question. One decider per decision.
3. **Interfaces and cadences** — how the two sides sync. A standing meeting cadence (biweekly or monthly), a shared backlog, a clear escalation path when the standing meeting is not enough.
4. **Shared metrics** — three to five metrics both sides are accountable for. For L&D: completion rate, assessment pass rate, cycle-time from content change to LMS live. For HR: in-scope roster accuracy, lapse rate, onboarding completion rate. For Internal Comms: spotlight cadence, reach, qualitative feedback on comms land.
5. **What this partnership does *not* cover** — explicit boundaries. This is the most-skipped and most-load-bearing section. "This partnership does not cover team-scope delivery (→ `ai-augmented-team-manager`), the control library (→ `senior-ai-governance-architect`), or legal opinions (→ counsel)."

The contract is signed and dated. Reviewed annually. Co-authored as a redline — the same redline-log pattern the policy work uses in [mod-103 Chapter 01](../mod-103-policy-co-authoring-with-governance/01-the-co-author-posture-and-the-redline-log.md).

## The HR scope boundary — the one that is hardest to hold

Three recurring HR asks are *not* in your scope, and pushing back on them in the contract is important.

- **"Can your team run our AI hiring assessment?"** No. AI-assisted hiring is a workforce-selection process with its own legal obligations (EEOC guidance in the US, Article 6 / high-risk system obligations in the EU, NYC Local Law 144, and others). It is the HR function's hiring process, with legal and governance co-authors. The enablement lead's team may provide training on how to use an approved tool in the hiring workflow; it does not run the workflow.
- **"Can your team deliver our mandatory-training workflow for AI misconduct?"** No. Disciplinary pathways are HR's; the enablement lead publishes completion data, HR runs the discipline. Mixing the two makes the enablement lead's team the enforcement arm, which destroys the credibility the program needs with operators.
- **"Can your team run the manager-training on how to use AI for performance reviews?"** This one is more nuanced. The *fluency content* (how to use AI to draft performance-review language) is in scope — it is a leadership-tier use case in the leadership playbook from [Chapter 04](04-role-based-playbooks-and-use-case-libraries.md). The *performance-review process itself* — its criteria, its governance, its documentation standards — is HR's. The contract names the split cleanly.

Hold the line in writing. These three asks will come up repeatedly in the first year.

## The internal comms lane — the one that is easiest to over-use

Internal comms will gladly amplify the program. The risk is over-amplifying — flooding the organization with AI announcements, which trains operators to tune out when the message matters.

The rule to write into the comms contract: **three messages per month maximum** to the full organization on AI topics, barring an incident (which has its own fast lane in [mod-109](../mod-109-ai-incident-response-and-enablement-side-rollback/README.md)). Everything else is targeted (to a function, to a region, to the leadership tier).

A monthly "AI update" that reliably lands on the first Monday is worth four frantic announcements scattered across the month.

## The L&D cycle-time negotiation

The hardest conversation with L&D is the one about cycle time. L&D's standard intake → design → review → publish cycle for new content is often measured in weeks or months. Your approved-tool envelope can change in days.

Three carve-outs to negotiate into the L&D contract:

- **Self-serve content** (prompt libraries, playbook entries, approved-tool-envelope updates) lives outside the LMS and is maintained on a wiki or equivalent. L&D is a reviewer, not the owner.
- **Spotlight content** moves through a fast review lane — 48 hours max from draft to published.
- **Formal curriculum** (foundational modules, assessments, LMS-bound content) runs the standard L&D cycle. Count this as the slow-moving layer; everything else moves faster.

This is the same hub-and-spoke pattern the operating model uses for intake ([mod-101 Chapter 03](../mod-101-role-and-the-org-wide-enablement-operating-model/03-authoring-the-enablement-operating-model.md)): formal pipeline for high-stakes artifacts, fast lanes for everything else.

## The partner failure modes

- **Partner-as-vendor.** Treating L&D as a service provider to buy courses from. Produces transactional output without shared accountability. The contract fixes this by naming shared metrics.
- **Partner-as-obstacle.** Treating L&D as a bureaucratic blocker. Produces end-runs around L&D that create audit holes later. The carve-outs fix this.
- **Partner-as-owner.** Letting HR or L&D own the program. Produces a program at their altitude, not yours, and the organizational signal that AI literacy is "an L&D initiative" rather than a strategic capability.
- **Partner-as-co-signer-only.** Getting the signature without getting the work. Produces a signed contract nobody operates against. The standing cadence meeting fixes this if it is held reliably.
- **No contract at all.** Producing a partnership on trust. Works until the first personnel change.

## When the partnership breaks

Contracts are written with the current counterparts; counterparts rotate. A new head of L&D arrives, a new VP of HR rotates in, a new internal comms lead comes from outside the organization. The contract is the thing you walk the new counterpart through in their first month — not the first six months.

Keep a short handover note at the top of each contract: *"In the last 12 months the biggest open items have been X, Y, Z. The last time this contract was revised was [date] and the revision was about [thing]."* The handover note is the audit the contract does on itself.

## Summary

- Four partners: L&D, HR, Internal Comms, and the champion network. Each brings a distinct contribution; name it before naming the ask.
- Team-scope delivery defers *down* to `ai-augmented-team-manager` (level 40). Publish the kit; let the manager deliver it to their team.
- Write a partnership contract per partner — shared goal, roles, interfaces and cadences, shared metrics, and explicit scope boundaries. Signed, dated, reviewed annually.
- HR asks on hiring assessments, AI-misconduct enforcement, and performance-review processes are usually *not* in your scope. Hold the line in writing.
- Negotiate cycle-time carve-outs with L&D so self-serve and spotlight content move faster than formal curriculum. Everything cannot run on L&D's standard cycle.
- Three messages per month maximum from internal comms to the full org on AI topics, barring an incident. Targeted messages are not capped.
- The contract is the artifact that survives counterpart turnover. Keep a handover note at the top so each new counterpart can get oriented in week one.
