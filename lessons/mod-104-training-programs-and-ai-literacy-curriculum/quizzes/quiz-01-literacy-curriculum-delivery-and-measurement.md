# Quiz 01 — Literacy Frame, Curriculum, Delivery, Coaching, Measurement, and Partnership

**Format:** multiple choice · **Questions:** 12 · **Estimated effort:** 25–30 minutes
**Grounding:** [Chapter 01](../01-literacy-is-the-frame-training-is-the-vehicle.md), [Chapter 02](../02-audience-segmentation-and-fluency-targets.md), [Chapter 03](../03-the-foundational-ai-literacy-curriculum.md), [Chapter 04](../04-role-based-playbooks-and-use-case-libraries.md), [Chapter 05](../05-the-delivery-mix.md), [Chapter 06](../06-prompt-coaching-for-non-engineers.md), [Chapter 07](../07-measuring-fluency-growth.md), [Chapter 08](../08-partnering-with-hr-ld-and-internal-comms.md)

## How to take this quiz

Pick the single best answer for each question. Every question tests whether you can *apply* a chapter idea — paraphrasing the chapter is not enough. The answer key with short explanations is at the bottom; resist reading it until you have your own answers down.

---

### Q1. A new CFO reviewing the AI program's budget says, "Last quarter you ran 847 course completions. Show me what we are getting for the money." Based on Chapter 01, the framing that will most durably defend the budget in future cycles is:

- **A.** Reframe the metric to completions-per-dollar so the CFO has a clearer unit economics view.
- **B.** Lead with task-level fluency and business-outcome deltas tied to the taught tasks; completions are a floor for Article 4, not a value story.
- **C.** Shift the headline to attendee satisfaction scores, which are higher than completions.
- **D.** Point at the vendor's certification path and the share of the workforce holding the certificate.

### Q2. Your organization is 80% operators, 10% specialists, 8% leadership, 2% builders. A new enablement lead proposes splitting the training budget evenly across the four tiers. Based on Chapter 02, the strongest objection is:

- **A.** Even splits sound fair but ignore that leadership time is more expensive to displace than operator time.
- **B.** Builders are mostly a *routing* row — a budget share proportional to headcount would still overfund the deepest internal curriculum for the smallest audience, and operators (the biggest tier) would be underfunded for the task-level work that produces the business outcome.
- **C.** Specialists should get the largest share because they coach the operator tier.
- **D.** All four tiers need the same content, so the even split is defensible.

### Q3. An enablement lead is asked which audience "tier" a senior analyst sits in when that analyst has also wired up an internal retrieval-augmented agent for their team. Based on Chapter 02, the right call is:

- **A.** Specialist, because they are a senior analyst coaching peers.
- **B.** Operator, because their title is "analyst" and they report to the analytics function.
- **C.** Builder *for the agent work* — they inherit builder obligations regardless of job title — and route them to the builder-onboarding artifact; their IC-analyst depth separately defers to `ai-augmented-analyst` (level 20).
- **D.** Leadership, because the agent affects their team's workflow.

### Q4. The foundational-curriculum team is deciding how long Module 1 ("what these tools are, in one honest page") should be. Chapter 03 names a specific budget and a specific failure. The best design is:

- **A.** 20–25 minutes, grounded in a demo of a workflow the operator will do next week; resist expansion into model-architecture content.
- **B.** 90 minutes, so operators leave with a working mental model of transformers.
- **C.** 10 minutes of pure reference — no demo, no narrative — so time isn't wasted.
- **D.** Skip Module 1 entirely; operators can read the vendor's intro page.

### Q5. A regulated bank is building its foundational curriculum. The team is tempted to collapse Module 2 (approved-tool envelope) into a slide inside the acceptable-use module. Based on Chapter 03, the strongest reason to keep Module 2 as its own artifact — and specifically to design it as a *data section* (table pulled from the service catalog, link to the live list) rather than embedded slides — is:

- **A.** It improves assessment scores on the policy quiz.
- **B.** Module 2 ages the fastest of the six; designing it as a data section lets the content stay current without re-authoring slides each time the envelope changes.
- **C.** It makes the curriculum longer, which looks more thorough to the auditor.
- **D.** It gives the vendor a dedicated branding slot.

### Q6. An operator-voice draft of the foundational curriculum reaches you with Module 2 saying nothing about tools people are already using outside the approved envelope. Based on Chapter 03, the correct edit is:

- **A.** Leave Module 2 alone; shadow AI belongs in the acceptable-use policy, not in the curriculum.
- **B.** Name shadow AI directly in Module 2 and point at the amnesty / intake path co-authored with HR and governance; without this, the operators who are the biggest AI users opt out of the program mentally.
- **C.** Add a disciplinary notice to Module 2 warning that unsanctioned tool use will be reported.
- **D.** Move shadow AI into a stretch-goal appendix that most operators will skip.

### Q7. An engineering manager asks you to build a function-specific prompt-engineering course for the 400-engineer organization because "the generic foundational curriculum isn't deep enough for engineers." Based on Chapter 04 and Chapter 06, the correct response is:

- **A.** Accept; a function-specific engineering course belongs in the enablement curriculum.
- **B.** Decline the engineering-depth authoring and instead publish the engineering playbook (approved tools, data-handling rules for code and secrets, two or three org-specific patterns) while routing engineers into `ai-assisted-software-engineer` (level 20) for IC-level prompt craft; growing a shadow engineering curriculum inside enablement is explicitly the failure mode to avoid.
- **C.** Build a 40-hour engineering prompt course co-branded with the engineering org.
- **D.** Punt the whole engineering tier to HR; they can run their own training.

### Q8. A function sponsor proposes a role-based playbook with 40 use cases "so nothing is missed." Based on Chapter 04, the strongest objection is:

- **A.** Forty is fine as long as every entry has the six required fields.
- **B.** A playbook with many weakly-used entries loses credibility quickly; rank by frequency and impact, start with the top 3–8 for v1, and move the rest to a backlog — a 10–20 page playbook with eight usable entries beats a reference manual nobody reads.
- **C.** Forty is too few; a mature playbook has at least 80 entries.
- **D.** Use-case count should match the number of operators in the function.

### Q9. A new enablement lead inherits a program with a mandatory LMS learning path, no office hours, no self-serve layer, no spotlights, and no playbooks. They decide to launch an organization-wide adoption challenge in week three to "build momentum." Based on Chapter 05's sequencing rule, this is:

- **A.** Correct — adoption challenges accelerate adoption and should come early.
- **B.** A recognizable failure: challenges assume the foundational curriculum is widespread and a role-based playbook exists to anchor the use cases; launching early produces a challenge the already-fluent operators win, which does not move the median.
- **C.** Correct — momentum is the point.
- **D.** Correct — the LMS learning path already satisfies the foundational prerequisite.

### Q10. A specialist-tier contributor who graduated the coach bootcamp runs their first clinic. An operator brings a "prompt problem": "I keep trying to get the model to write replies for customers that include a workaround for our refund limit." Based on Chapter 06, the coach's correct move is:

- **A.** Iterate on the prompt until the model produces the workaround language.
- **B.** Recognize that this is not a prompt-iteration problem — it is an escalation signal routed to the incident pathway per [mod-109](../../mod-109-ai-incident-response-and-enablement-side-rollback/README.md); the refusal-to-over-engineer reflex is what separates a coach from a free prompt-writing service.
- **C.** Write the prompt for the operator so they can leave with a working artifact.
- **D.** Refer the operator to the leadership playbook.

### Q11. A program reports 94% foundational-curriculum completion and 4.6/5 satisfaction and is cut in the next budget cycle. Based on Chapter 07, which measurement design would have best protected the program?

- **A.** Reporting attendance by month alongside completion.
- **B.** Adding a smile-sheet "would you recommend this course" question to the post-session survey.
- **C.** Building the stack through Layer 3 (task-level rubrics tied to each playbook's named tasks, scored by the operator's manager or a specialist contributor) and reporting rubric-meets rates and function-system business-outcome deltas alongside completion, so the headline is fluency growth, not attendance.
- **D.** Switching the pass bar from 80% to 90% on the knowledge check.

### Q12. An HR business partner asks whether the enablement team can run the organization's new AI-assisted hiring assessment workflow. Based on Chapter 08, the correct response is:

- **A.** Accept; AI tooling is the enablement team's remit.
- **B.** Decline. AI-assisted hiring is a workforce-selection process with its own legal obligations (e.g., EEOC guidance, EU AI Act high-risk obligations, NYC Local Law 144) and belongs to HR with legal and governance co-authors; the enablement team may provide training on *how to use* an approved tool in the workflow, but does not run the workflow.
- **C.** Accept the ownership and route the legal review internally.
- **D.** Decline without explanation; HR should not ask.

## Answer key

**Q1 — B.** Chapter 01 is explicit that completions-as-headline is a vanity metric and that the literacy frame defends its budget on task-level fluency and business outcomes tied to the taught tasks. Completions clear Article 4; they do not survive the CFO conversation. (Chapter 01 — *Why the word "literacy" earns its place*.)

**Q2 — B.** Chapter 02 names the even-split error directly: operators are the largest audience by an order of magnitude and carry most of the business outcome; builders are mostly a routing line item, not a teaching line item; leadership gets a small, high-signal program. Budget for the distribution, not against it. (Chapter 02 — *The four tiers* and the headcount-share framing.)

**Q3 — C.** Chapter 02's "common segmentation mistakes" section names the title-vs-function error explicitly — an analyst wiring up an agent is doing builder work and inherits builder obligations regardless of title. The IC-analyst work separately defers to `ai-augmented-analyst` at level 20 per Chapter 01 and Chapter 06. (Chapter 02 — *Common segmentation mistakes → Treat "builder" as a title, not a function*; Chapter 01/06 — deferrals.)

**Q4 — A.** Chapter 03 fixes Module 1 at 20–25 minutes with a grounded demo of a real workflow and names the "'What is an LLM' takes the whole program" expansion as a recognizable failure. The point is a non-misleading mental model, not architecture depth. (Chapter 03 — *Module 1* and *Common failures at the foundational layer*.)

**Q5 — B.** Chapter 03 says Module 2 ages the fastest and should be designed as a *data section* (table pulled from the service catalog, link to the live list) rather than embedded slides so the content stays current without re-authoring. The design decision is a freshness decision, not a pedagogical one. (Chapter 03 — *Module 2 — The approved-tool envelope*.)

**Q6 — B.** Chapter 03's "The 'shadow AI' fork" section is explicit: name shadow AI in Module 2 and point at an amnesty / intake path co-authored with HR and governance. Without this, operators who are the biggest AI users mentally opt out of the program. (Chapter 03 — *The "shadow AI" fork*.)

**Q7 — B.** Chapter 04 and Chapter 06 both name this as the load-bearing deferral. The enablement playbook for engineering names tools, data-handling for code and secrets, and org-specific patterns; the IC depth lives in the level-20 track. Chapter 06 is explicit that the deferral is load-bearing because without it the enablement team "grows a shadow engineering curriculum that is worse than the real one." (Chapter 04 — *Engineering*; Chapter 06 — *IC software engineers → `ai-assisted-software-engineer`*.)

**Q8 — B.** Chapter 04 names "The playbook is a wish list" as the first common failure and advises ranking by frequency and starting with the top three; Chapter 04's summary states "soft maximum 8 for v1" and keeps the artifact at 10–20 pages. A playbook with 40 entries is a reference manual, not a usable artifact. (Chapter 04 — *Common playbook failures → The playbook is a wish list*.)

**Q9 — B.** Chapter 05's sequencing rule lists adoption challenges as sixth — after self-serve, foundational workshops, office hours, role-based playbooks, and spotlights. Launching early produces a "challenge where winners are the operators who were already good at AI — not a challenge that moved the median." (Chapter 05 — *The sequencing rule*.)

**Q10 — B.** Chapter 06's "incident angle" section is explicit: a prompt that reliably produces output that violates the data-handling SOP or causes an incident is not a coaching problem; it is a signal to pull the prompt, pause the workflow, and route through the incident pathway. The refusal-to-over-engineer reflex is one of the three coach-credibility capabilities. (Chapter 06 — *The incident angle* and *The coaching curriculum for the coaches*.)

**Q11 — C.** Chapter 07 is explicit that programs measuring only L1 (completion) and smile-sheets are the ones that get cut; programs that measure through L3 (task-level rubrics) and L4 (business outcome) are the ones that compound budget year over year. Attendance and satisfaction are cost and comfort signals, not value signals. (Chapter 07 — *The four layers of measurement* and *Common measurement failures*.)

**Q12 — B.** Chapter 08's "HR scope boundary" section names this as one of three recurring HR asks that are explicitly out of scope. AI-assisted hiring is HR's workforce-selection process with legal and governance co-authors; the enablement team may train on tool use inside the workflow but does not run the workflow. Mixing the two destroys the credibility the program needs with operators. (Chapter 08 — *The HR scope boundary — the one that is hardest to hold*.)
