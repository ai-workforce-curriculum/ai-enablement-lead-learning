# The Delivery Mix

> The content is only as good as the vehicles that carry it. A perfectly designed curriculum delivered entirely through one-hour webinars will die inside six months. A delivery mix is the deliberate combination of vehicles — synchronous, asynchronous, cohort, self-serve, coaching, event — that together fit the organization's rhythms and get the content into operators' hands at the moment they need it. This chapter is the menu and the rules for choosing.

## The six vehicles — and when each earns its slot

Six delivery vehicles cover the full menu. Every program uses most of them in some mix. Picking only one or two is a sign the mix is under-designed.

### 1 — Workshops (deep, scheduled)

A workshop is a 90-minute to full-day block, cohort-based, with a facilitator, hands-on exercises, and a defined learning objective. Workshops are the right vehicle when:

- The content is behavior change, not information transfer (Modules 3–5 of the foundational curriculum).
- The audience benefits from working on real examples from their own role.
- The learning requires a facilitator who can read the room and course-correct.

Not every topic deserves a workshop. The common failure is running a workshop on what should have been a 10-minute reference card. Reserve the workshop vehicle for the content the organization most needs to change behavior on — generally the policy-into-practice modules and the role-based playbook rollouts.

Cadence: usually a *cohort* per function per quarter, in the first year of a curriculum rollout. Dropping below quarterly means the content gets stale; rising above monthly usually means a bigger problem is being masked as a training gap.

### 2 — Office hours (the standing drop-in)

Office hours are a recurring, low-friction slot where operators bring real problems to a CoE member or a champion. The value is not in the pedagogy; the value is in the fact that the operator does not have to wait for the next cohort to get help.

Office hours work when:

- They are *standing* — same day, same time, every week, without exception. Operators plan around a reliable slot; they do not plan around a sporadic one.
- The host is credible to the audience — a champion or specialist from that function, not a generic trainer.
- The format is *drop-in* — no pre-registration required, no agenda required, bring the actual problem you are stuck on.

The killer anti-pattern: office hours advertised but routinely cancelled or empty. Better to run one slot at high reliability than three slots that sometimes exist.

Cadence: at minimum, one org-wide office hour per week (the "general" slot) plus one per major function (sales, service, legal, etc.) per week once the role-based playbooks have rolled out. The org-wide slot can be one hour; function slots can be 30 minutes.

### 3 — Lunch-and-learns (and other short-form sessions)

A 30–45 minute session, usually at midday, usually optional, usually broadcast or hybrid, usually one topic with light interaction. Different from a workshop by format; different from office hours by structure (planned topic, presenter, often a recording for later).

Lunch-and-learns work when:

- The topic is short enough to make the time slot meaningful.
- The audience is cross-function (not a single role).
- The attendee commitment is self-selection, not mandate.

Common failures: using a lunch-and-learn to carry mandatory content (operators who need the content most are the ones who skip optional sessions); scheduling topics the CoE wants to talk about rather than topics the audience is asking about.

Cadence: biweekly is a sustainable rhythm. Weekly burns out the audience and the speakers. Monthly loses momentum. Mix the topics: deep-dive on a new tool, a function spotlight, a leadership Q&A, a research briefing on an external development.

### 4 — Bootcamps (intensive)

A bootcamp is a condensed, cohort-based program running one to five days (or across several weeks in short daily sessions) with a specific capability as the outcome. Bootcamps are expensive — operator time, facilitator time, materials — and should be reserved for the specialist and builder tiers.

Bootcamps work when:

- The capability requires concentrated practice, not spaced learning.
- The cohort is small (15–25) and selected.
- The outcome is a named artifact or demonstrated capability the organization needs on day one after the bootcamp.

Specialist-tier bootcamps shape the champion network ([mod-105](../mod-105-champion-networks-and-change-management/README.md)). Builder-tier bootcamps typically route operators *out* to an external vendor program or an internal partnership with engineering — your organization usually does not run the builder bootcamp in-house.

Cadence: twice a year for specialist cohorts. On-demand for builder onboarding. Not more; the format loses its power if it happens too often.

### 5 — Adoption challenges (the time-boxed campaign)

A short, named campaign — two to six weeks — where the organization focuses on using AI for a specific set of tasks, with leaderboards, prizes, public showcases, or team-vs-team structures. Think "hackathon" for operators, not for builders.

Adoption challenges work when:

- The foundational curriculum has rolled out broadly enough that participants have a baseline.
- The challenge is anchored to *real use cases from the playbooks*, not to novelty tasks.
- Leadership is visibly participating, not just funding.

Common failures: challenges that reward volume ("most prompts submitted") over quality — operators optimize for the metric and incident rates spike. Design the metric around *a shipped artifact of real value*, not against activity volume.

Cadence: one major challenge per year is plenty. Two to three smaller function-level challenges on top. More than that and the term "challenge" loses its meaning.

### 6 — Use-case spotlights (the showcase)

A short, often video-based, artifact that shows a real operator using AI for a real task in their own role, with the artifact and the outcome on display. These travel through the organization's internal comms channels, the champion network, and the lunch-and-learn program.

Spotlights work when:

- They name a real person, a real function, and a real outcome.
- They have been vetted — the data handling and the escalation path are correct.
- They are *not* vendor marketing. A spotlight that could be in a tool vendor's advertising is not credible internally.

Spotlights are the lightweight vehicle that stitches the heavier vehicles together. They give the champion network material to share. They give the lunch-and-learn program its most-watched slots. They give leadership the "this is what's happening in your org" artifact for the all-hands.

Cadence: one spotlight every two to four weeks, pulled from the use-case library ([Chapter 04](04-role-based-playbooks-and-use-case-libraries.md)). Pipeline flow means always having one in production and two in review.

### 7 — Self-serve resources (the always-on layer)

Everything that does not require live delivery lives here. The acceptable-use policy. The data-handling SOP. The approved-tool envelope. The prompt and agent libraries from each playbook. The office hours schedule. The recorded lunch-and-learns. The escalation roster.

Self-serve is not a fallback; it is the vehicle that carries the largest share of literacy value because it is the only vehicle operators consult in the moment of use. Underinvest here and the live vehicles compensate by running more often, badly.

The design criteria:

- **Findability.** One place, well-indexed, searchable, linked from the approved-tool UI if possible.
- **Freshness.** A changelog on each page. A dated "last reviewed." An owner.
- **Operator voice.** Not legal voice, not governance voice, not executive voice. The operator opens this page while mid-task; the text respects that.

Self-serve has a dedicated owner on the CoE team (usually the operations analyst from [mod-101 Chapter 03](../mod-101-role-and-the-org-wide-enablement-operating-model/03-authoring-the-enablement-operating-model.md)). Without the owner, the self-serve layer rots fastest of all the vehicles.

## Mapping vehicles to content

Not every piece of content fits every vehicle. The mapping below is a starting shape; adapt to what your organization actually does.

| Content | Primary vehicle | Secondary vehicle |
|---|---|---|
| Foundational curriculum Modules 1–2 | Self-serve (async) | Onboarding workshop |
| Foundational curriculum Modules 3–5 | Workshop (cohort) | Recorded for refreshers |
| Foundational curriculum Module 6 | Self-serve reference | Reminder in office hours |
| Role-based playbook rollout | Workshop (function cohort) | Office hours for follow-up |
| New use-case announcement | Use-case spotlight | Lunch-and-learn |
| Prompt library expansion | Self-serve library | Office hours |
| Champion training | Bootcamp | Community of practice |
| Leadership briefing | Executive workshop (90 min) | Scorecard review ([mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md)) |
| New policy change | Workshop + mandatory refresh | Use-case spotlight for changed use |
| Adoption milestone (department reaches X%) | Spotlight | All-hands callout |

## The annual delivery rhythm

A sustainable program has a rhythm by quarter. One shape that recurs:

- **Q1 — Foundation cycle.** New-hire cohorts for the foundational curriculum. Playbook refresh for the two functions whose playbooks are most stale. First use-case challenge of the year.
- **Q2 — Role depth.** Role-based playbook workshops for two to three functions. Specialist bootcamp. First use-case spotlight series.
- **Q3 — Adoption push.** Adoption challenge. Second spotlight series. Mid-year leadership scorecard review.
- **Q4 — Measurement and plan.** Annual literacy assessment. Specialist bootcamp. Curriculum rev for next year. Spotlight series on the year's biggest shifts.

Office hours, lunch-and-learns, and self-serve run continuously across the year.

Q1 and Q4 are the heaviest for the CoE team; Q2 and Q3 are the heaviest for operators. The two team shapes need to be sequenced — do not pile adoption challenges on top of a curriculum rev.

## The sequencing rule

The vehicles stack in a specific order. Running a later vehicle before the earlier one is a recognizable failure.

1. **Self-serve first.** Before you run anything live, the self-serve layer exists at a minimum viable level. Operators who want to self-start can.
2. **Foundational workshops second.** The workshop series launches once self-serve is in place; it is more credible when it points to a real reference library.
3. **Office hours third.** Office hours light up once a critical mass of operators has completed the foundational curriculum; otherwise office hours attract the wrong questions.
4. **Role-based playbooks and workshops fourth.** The role workshops assume the foundational curriculum.
5. **Spotlights and lunch-and-learns fifth.** These need real shipped use cases to spotlight.
6. **Adoption challenges sixth.** Challenges assume a baseline of fluency in the population.
7. **Bootcamps seventh.** Bootcamps assume a champion network that can absorb the graduates.

Each vehicle has prerequisites. Launching a challenge before the foundational curriculum is widespread produces a challenge where winners are the operators who were already good at AI — not a challenge that moved the median.

## What a healthy delivery mix looks like after year one

By the end of the first full cycle, the mix is visible as a single dashboard:

- Foundational curriculum completion by in-scope population (target: 95%+).
- Playbooks live, by function.
- Weekly office hours running, by function, with attendance.
- Spotlights published in the quarter.
- Bootcamp cohorts graduated and still active in the champion network.
- Adoption challenges completed and the use cases that shipped from them.
- Self-serve page views, search queries, and top-viewed pages.

The dashboard belongs to the operations analyst and flows up into the exec scorecard in [mod-106](../mod-106-outcome-measurement-and-executive-reporting/README.md).

## Common delivery-mix failures

- **One-vehicle programs.** "We have a learning path in the LMS; that's our program." The LMS is one vehicle. It cannot carry a literacy program alone.
- **Over-scheduling.** Every week has three events; attendance drops; the CoE becomes "that calendar invite I decline." Fewer events at higher reliability outperform more events at inconsistent quality.
- **Unowned self-serve.** The self-serve pages were set up in year one, never touched again. Within 90 days the information is wrong somewhere; within a year the pages are a liability.
- **Vehicles without feedback loops.** Workshops that collect no post-session feedback, office hours with no attendance log, spotlights with no view count. Each vehicle needs a signal that it is working.
- **No sequencing.** Launching the adoption challenge in week three. Running a specialist bootcamp before the foundational curriculum has rolled out. Each vehicle has prerequisites; respect them.

## Summary

- Six primary vehicles plus self-serve. Pick the mix deliberately; no single vehicle carries a program.
- Each vehicle has prerequisites — launch them in order (self-serve → foundational workshops → office hours → role workshops → spotlights and lunch-and-learns → challenges → bootcamps). Launching out of order produces predictable failures.
- Self-serve is not a fallback; it is the vehicle operators consult *at the moment of use*. Underinvest here and everything else compensates poorly.
- Annual rhythm has a shape by quarter: foundation, role depth, adoption push, measurement and plan. Office hours, lunch-and-learns, and self-serve run continuously.
- A healthy delivery mix is visible as a single dashboard the operations analyst owns.
