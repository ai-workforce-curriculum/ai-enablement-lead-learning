# The Level-45 AI Enablement Lead and the Scope Contract

> The hardest part of this role isn't what you do. It's what you *don't* do — and being able to say why, in one page, to a CAIO, a CISO, and an HR partner who will each try to hand you work that isn't yours.

## Why "scope contract" is the first artifact

The AI Enablement Lead sits at level 45 in the AI Workforce family. It is the first level on this ladder where the unit of work is *the organization*, not a team. That shift pulls in work from four adjacent ladders:

- Team managers want you to run their team playbook.
- Governance wants you to own the policy.
- Security wants you to own the DLP gap.
- The executive office wants you to own the strategy.

Each one is partially right. The job is to say — crisply, in writing — which part is yours, which part isn't, and what the hand-off looks like for the rest. That written answer is the **scope contract**. Before you choose a CoE shape, before you write a single policy, before you spin up the first pilot, write the scope contract and get it signed.

## The adjacent roles the contract is written against

You will be mistaken for each of these, repeatedly, often by senior people who are not clear on the distinction. Internalize the differences until you can draw them on a whiteboard without notes.

### `ai-augmented-team-manager` — level 40, one level below, single-team scope

What they own: AI adoption for a 5–30-person team. The team's use-case backlog, the team's playbook, the team's weekly standup rituals. They hire for AI fluency on their team. They measure *their* team's productivity delta.

Where you overlap: training content, playbook templates, the champion program.

Where you diverge: *scope*. A team manager running AI adoption for one team is not you. You are the person they come to when they want the playbook, the training package, the policy they can point at, and the budget line to send a team member to a bootcamp. You do not run one team's adoption — you run the system that makes any team's adoption possible.

Common mistake: being handed "team manager plus" scope — a single team's AI program with a bigger title. Say no in the contract.

### `senior-ai-governance-architect` — level 50, AI Governance family, one level above

What they own: the control library the governance program plugs in. They design the taxonomy — risk classes, control types, evidence requirements, policy templates, audit lineage. They architect; they do not necessarily operate.

Where you overlap: policy templates (you co-author on their taxonomy), intake questionnaires (you run the enablement side; they design the schema).

Where you diverge: *architecture vs. operation*. They design the controls. You run the operating model the controls plug into — the intake, the training, the rollout artifact set, the champion network, the measurement scorecard. If you find yourself redesigning the control taxonomy, you are drifting into their work. Call them in instead.

### `head-of-ai-governance` — level 60, AI Governance family

What they own: the governance program at enterprise scope. Board-facing governance reporting, regulator engagement, the AI governance council chair seat, the enterprise risk posture. They own policy *ownership*; you *co-author*.

Where you overlap: the AI governance council (you attend as the enablement liaison), policy drafts (you draft, they ratify), incident escalation.

Where you diverge: *co-author vs. owner*. If a policy disagreement escalates to the general counsel or the board, that's their meeting, not yours. If the regulator wants a conversation, that's their meeting, not yours. You feed them evidence; they carry the account.

### `chief-ai-officer` — level 70, executive scope

What they own: enterprise AI strategy. The P&L of the AI portfolio, M&A on AI companies, the board narrative, the three-year AI bet. They set the direction; you deliver the operating system that lets the direction happen.

Where you overlap: the executive scorecard (you build it; they use it to talk to the board), budget asks (you build the business case; they decide the ask).

Where you diverge: *delivery vs. strategy*. You do not set the three-year AI bet. You build the capacity to execute whichever bet is set.

## The one-page scope contract — what it must contain

Keep it to one page. If it's longer, you've described a job description, not a contract. The contract has five sections:

1. **What this role owns** — the enablement operating model at organization scope: tool selection & rollout, policy co-authoring, training & AI literacy, champion network & change management, outcome measurement & exec reporting, enablement-side incident response, commissioning of internal tools, CoE operations.
2. **What this role does not own** — control-library architecture (→ `senior-ai-governance-architect`), governance program leadership (→ `head-of-ai-governance`), enterprise AI strategy (→ `chief-ai-officer`), engineered risk / red-team evidence (→ `ai-risk-engineer`), the build of internal agents and tools (→ `applied-ai-engineer` / `agentic-ai-engineer`), team-scope adoption (→ `ai-augmented-team-manager`), legal opinion, contract execution, SSO/IAM, SOC-side investigation.
3. **Who signs off on what** — a short RACI pointing at policy, rollout, training, exec scorecard, and incident response.
4. **The decisions this role makes unilaterally** — three to five decisions you can make without a council. Common set: which tools enter the approved-tool envelope for pilot; which champions are promoted or retired; the training-curriculum calendar; the service-catalog version; which rollout gets the kill switch pulled.
5. **The decisions this role escalates** — three to five decisions you *must* route up. Common set: policy that conflicts with legal opinion; incidents that meet regulator-notification criteria; any multi-million-dollar vendor commit; terminations tied to AI misuse; anything that touches the board narrative.

## Why one page, and why signed

Every month you are in the role, something or someone will try to grow the job in a direction that reports no value and costs enormous time. A short, signed contract is the thing you point at. Without it, the role becomes "AI person" — which is not a role, it is a complaint queue.

## Summary

- The role is level 45, AI Workforce family, organization scope.
- You sit between the team manager (one level down) and the governance / architect / executive roles (one or two levels up and sideways in the Governance family).
- The first artifact of the role is the one-page scope contract — what you own, what you don't, who signs, what you decide unilaterally, what you escalate.
- Write it, get it signed, and return to it the first time someone tries to hand you work that belongs to another ladder.
